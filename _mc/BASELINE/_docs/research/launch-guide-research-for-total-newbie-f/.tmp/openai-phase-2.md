# Launch Guide – Failure Mode Analysis (2026)

New founders must anticipate what can **go wrong** when launching their first mobile/web product. This report examines failure modes across three key workstreams – **Growth Loops**, **Onboarding**, and **Testing on Windows** – with evidence from industry reports and post-mortems. Each finding includes the claim, supporting evidence, and an assessed confidence level.

## 1. Growth Loops – In-App Reviews & Referrals (Failure Modes)

### In-App Review Prompts

- **“Review Gating” Triggers App Store Rejection:** **Claim:** Showing a pre-prompt (“Enjoying the app?”) that sends only happy users to the official review dialog while shunting unhappy users to feedback is explicitly banned. **Evidence:** Apple prohibited this **review gating** practice in 2017 and Google adopted a similar stance ([storelit.co](https://storelit.co/blog/how-to-get-more-app-store-reviews#:~:text=,prompt%20is%20a%20guideline%20violation)). It’s seen as manipulating ratings by filtering negative feedback out of public reviews ([storelit.co](https://storelit.co/blog/how-to-get-more-app-store-reviews#:~:text=,before%20it%20reaches%20the%20store)). **Confidence:** High – *Documented in Apple/Google guidelines and cited by multiple sources.*  

- **Custom Rating Prompts Violate Policies:** **Claim:** Using a homemade in-app dialog to mimic the App Store review popup (instead of the official API) risks rejection. **Evidence:** Apple’s guidelines require using the `SKStoreReviewController` API for in-app ratings; mimicking the system UI is a guideline violation ([storelit.co](https://storelit.co/blog/how-to-get-more-app-store-reviews#:~:text=,prompt%20is%20a%20guideline%20violation)). No incentives may be offered in exchange for reviews ([storelit.co](https://storelit.co/blog/how-to-get-more-app-store-reviews#:~:text=,prompt%20is%20a%20guideline%20violation)). **Confidence:** High – *Enforced by App Store review rules.*  

- **Over-Prompting is Throttled or Wasted:** **Claim:** Both Apple and Google limit how frequently rating prompts appear, so naive implementations that call the API too often either get ignored or irritate users. **Evidence:** Apple’s `SKStoreReviewController` will display at most **3 prompts per user per year**, silently ignoring extra calls beyond that quota ([storelit.co](https://storelit.co/blog/how-to-get-more-app-store-reviews#:~:text=The%20hard%20limit%3A%20Apple%20will,that%20user%20until%20next%20year)). Apple may also suppress prompts based on its own timing heuristics, so calls made at suboptimal moments might not show anything ([storelit.co](https://storelit.co/blog/how-to-get-more-app-store-reviews#:~:text=There%20is%20a%20subtlety%20here,timing%20in%20the%20next%20section)). Google’s Play in-app review API has **undisclosed frequency limits** – the system decides when to show the review dialog, often allowing more prompts for engaged users but unpredictably ([storelit.co](https://storelit.co/blog/how-to-get-more-app-store-reviews#:~:text=per%20prompt%20shown)). **Confidence:** High – *Platform behavior confirmed by documentation and developer reports.*  

- **Bad Timing Undermines Reviews:** **Claim:** Triggering a review request at the wrong time (e.g. after a crash, during onboarding, or when the user is frustrated) can waste a precious prompt and even prompt negative feedback. **Evidence:** With only three chances per year on iOS, using one at a **“bad moment”** is a **strategic error** that cannot be undone until the next year ([storelit.co](https://storelit.co/blog/how-to-get-more-app-store-reviews#:~:text=precious,that%20user%20until%20next%20year)). Even under the limit, Apple might not show a prompt if the timing seems inappropriate ([storelit.co](https://storelit.co/blog/how-to-get-more-app-store-reviews#:~:text=There%20is%20a%20subtlety%20here,may%20suppress%20some%20of%20them)). **Confidence:** High – *Supported by Apple’s documented behavior and best-practice guides.*  

- **Ignoring Native APIs on Android:** **Claim:** On Android, not using the official **In-App Review API** (e.g. sending users out to Play Store manually) misses out on the seamless flow and may lead to fewer reviews. **Evidence:** The Play Core in-app review appears as a **native overlay** within the app, making it lower friction and yielding higher completion rates than external redirects ([storelit.co](https://storelit.co/blog/how-to-get-more-app-store-reviews#:~:text=Google%27s%20In,completion%20rates%20per%20prompt%20shown)). Failing to integrate it means more users will drop off before leaving feedback. **Confidence:** Medium – *Logical consequence backed by UX studies (lower friction = more completions).*  

### Referral Programs

- **Fake Referrals & Fraud Inflate Metrics:** **Claim:** A poorly guarded referral program can be hijacked by fake accounts and self-referrals, leading to bogus growth and wasted rewards. **Evidence:** *Self-referral fraud* is common – fraudsters create multiple fake accounts (using throwaway emails, VPNs, device spoofing) to refer themselves and claim signup bonuses ([www.prefinery.com](https://www.prefinery.com/blog/referral-fraud-types-and-prevention/#:~:text=Self)). This yields *“low-quality accounts that rarely convert”*, skewing customer-acquisition metrics and draining the reward budget ([www.prefinery.com](https://www.prefinery.com/blog/referral-fraud-types-and-prevention/#:~:text=Referral%20fraud%20is%20a%20growing,look%20effective%20when%20they%E2%80%99re%20not)) ([www.prefinery.com](https://www.prefinery.com/blog/referral-fraud-types-and-prevention/#:~:text=One%20common%20scheme%20involves%20self,months%2C%20credits%2C%20or%20cash%20payouts)). **Confidence:** High – *Numerous case studies and anti-fraud vendors document this pattern.*  

- **Incentive Misalignment (Freebie Chasers):** **Claim:** Generous referral rewards not tied to genuine usage can attract reward hunters who don’t become long-term users. **Evidence:** For example, a SaaS offering “1 free month for every referred friend” saw *thousands of fake signups* generated by a handful of users exploiting the promo ([www.prefinery.com](https://www.prefinery.com/blog/referral-fraud-types-and-prevention/#:~:text=numbers%20with%20low,churn%20spikes%2C%20revealing%20the%20fraud)). Many churned right after the free period, spiking short-term signups but hurting retention ([www.prefinery.com](https://www.prefinery.com/blog/referral-fraud-types-and-prevention/#:~:text=numbers%20with%20low,churn%20spikes%2C%20revealing%20the%20fraud)). **Confidence:** High – *Well-known outcome when rewards are too easy to game.*  

- **Public Code Leaks Undermine Referrals:** **Claim:** If referral codes or promo links intended for personal invites leak publicly (e.g. posted on coupon sites or forums), they can trigger a flood of unqualified signups. **Evidence:** In one incident, a startup’s private “friends and family 50% off” code was shared broadly online, leading to thousands of discounted signups with high churn and low conversion after the discount ended ([www.prefinery.com](https://www.prefinery.com/blog/referral-fraud-types-and-prevention/#:~:text=Coupon%20and%20Public%20Link%20Abuse)) ([www.prefinery.com](https://www.prefinery.com/blog/referral-fraud-types-and-prevention/#:~:text=For%20instance%2C%20a%20U,after%20the%20discount%20period%20ended)). This *coupon abuse* not only cuts revenue but also undermines the program’s integrity. **Confidence:** High – *Observed in marketing post-mortems.*  

- **Lack of Basic Fraud Controls:** **Claim:** Solo founders often launch referral schemes without any anti-fraud measures, making abuse trivial. **Evidence:** Common-sense checks like **email/IP validation, device fingerprinting, CAPTCHAs, or delaying rewards** until a referred user performs a meaningful action (e.g. a purchase or active use) are minimal safeguards ([www.prefinery.com](https://www.prefinery.com/blog/referral-fraud-types-and-prevention/#:~:text=%2A%20Common%20fraud%20tactics%3A%20Self,like%20payments%20or%20active%20engagement)) ([www.prefinery.com](https://www.prefinery.com/blog/referral-fraud-types-and-prevention/#:~:text=To%20combat%20this%2C%20consider%20requiring,before%20issuing%20rewards)). Without these, one person can repeatedly refer themselves and instantly reap rewards. **Confidence:** High – *Aligns with anti-fraud best practices; omission is easily exploited.*  

- **Overestimating Viral K-Factor:** **Claim:** There’s a risk in assuming a referral program will drive self-sustaining viral growth (k-factor ≥ 1) – in reality, most products never achieve that, and referrals only boost growth marginally. **Evidence:** Outside of a few famed cases (e.g. Dropbox’s referral loop yielding a **60% sign-up lift** ([signupdrop.com](https://signupdrop.com/#:~:text=Seven%20companies%20with%20documented%2C%20cited,signup%20flow%20decisions))), the majority of referral programs produce *k < 1*, meaning each user brings in less than one new user on average. Viral loops rarely overcome product-market fit issues. Rolling out referrals too early – before users truly love the product – can result in wasted development effort with little uptake. **Confidence:** Medium – *Supported by industry benchmarks and growth expert observations, though exact k-factor varies by product.*  

- **Premature Optimization vs. Core Product:** **Claim:** Building a complex referral system too soon can distract a founder from fixing core product value. **Evidence:** Growth advisors note that referrals only work if the product’s retention and satisfaction are strong; otherwise, referred users won’t stick around either. If the user base is very small, time spent on referral features might be better spent improving the product to achieve **organic** advocacy first. (No direct citation – **Confidence:** Medium, *logical deduction from growth frameworks like AARRR where Activation/Retention precede Referral*.)  

## 2. Onboarding – Signup & Activation (Failure Modes)

- **Signup Walls Cause Drop-Off:** **Claim:** Forcing new users to register *before* they experience any value (a “signup wall”) often leads to most potential users quitting early. **Evidence:** Industry research shows **60–80% median drop-off** during signup flows ([signupdrop.com](https://signupdrop.com/#:~:text=60)). Every additional field or step in the form can cut conversion by ~10 percentage points on average ([signupdrop.com](https://signupdrop.com/#:~:text=)). For example, requiring a phone number or email verification too early can drive users away before they solve their problem. **Confidence:** High – *Backed by aggregated data from multiple UX studies.*  

- **Missing Guest Mode Hurts Adoption:** **Claim:** Not offering a **guest** or **anonymous trial mode** is a growth killer, as many users want to “try before they buy/commit.” **Evidence:** A Firebase onboarding study notes that a compulsory account signup on first launch creates a frustrating experience: *“most people give up… the developer accidentally hurt the growth potential of their app.”* ([firebase.blog](https://firebase.blog/posts/2023/07/best-practices-for-anonymous-authentication/#:~:text=But%20instead%20of%20giving%20new,growth%20potential%20of%20their%20app)) Apps that allow initial exploration (e.g. browsing content or using basic features without sign-in) remove this hurdle. **Confidence:** High – *User experience research and developer advocates strongly emphasize early value delivery.*  

- **Anonymous Account “Linking” Pitfalls:** **Claim:** If guest users aren’t **seamlessly transitioned** to a permanent account, they risk losing data or continuity. **Evidence:** Developers often create a temporary anonymous account (to store user data) and later link it to the user’s new login (email, social, etc.). A common failure mode is mishandling this linking – e.g., creating a *new* account on sign-up instead of linking, causing the user’s prior data (from the guest session) to be orphaned. Firebase’s best practices highlight linking anon credentials to avoid data loss (source: Firebase Blog, 2023). **Confidence:** Medium – *Known pitfall referenced in platform docs and forums.*  

- **Over-Collecting User Info Too Early:** **Claim:** Asking for extensive personal details up front (before the user has derived value) deters completion and can violate privacy norms, especially for younger users. **Evidence:** Best practices in progressive profiling suggest only requesting info that’s immediately needed; unnecessary early questions (full profile, preferences, etc.) act as “friction” that can be moved to later in the user journey. For teen/minor audiences, **collecting extra data can raise compliance issues** – e.g., COPPA (U.S.) and GDPR-K (EU) limit data collection from users under 13–16 without parental consent. Several apps have faced legal action for not gating underage data (e.g. TikTok’s 2019 FTC fine for COPPA violations). **Confidence:** High – *Supported by UX research and legal requirements (COPPA/GDPR-K are enforced law).*  

- **Failure to Include Age Gates (Youth Apps):** **Claim:** If your product targets minors or could attract them, not building an age check or parental consent flow is a serious oversight. **Evidence:** Regulations like **COPPA** mandate verifiable parental consent for under-13 users. Apps that ignore this have been fined or removed. For example, the FTC has issued multi-million dollar fines to popular apps for mishandling children’s data. Apple’s guidelines also require apps in the “Kids” category to implement age-appropriate gating and privacy safeguards. **Confidence:** High – *Legally critical; evidenced by enforcement actions in the industry.*  

- **Misidentifying Activation Metrics:** **Claim:** Some founders fail to define or track a clear **activation event**, instead focusing only on sign-up counts. This is a strategic blind spot – you might be acquiring users who never get to the “aha moment.” **Evidence:** Growth frameworks (e.g., AARRR metrics) stress that *Activation* is typically measured by a specific action that correlates with retained users (such as completing a first lesson, making one purchase, etc.). If you don’t measure this, you can’t know if your onboarding is truly effective. Companies often set target activation rates (e.g. 20-40% of new signups achieve the key action within N days, depending on industry) to gauge health. Neglecting this can lead to falsely optimistic user counts while actual engagement is low. **Confidence:** Medium – *Widely accepted best practice in growth analytics, though exact targets vary.*  

- **Unoptimized Progressive Profiling:** **Claim:** Balancing what data to ask at signup versus later is tricky – a failure mode is either asking too much (causing drop-off) or asking too little (lacking info when needed). **Evidence:** A decision framework for progressive profiling suggests deferring non-critical questions until the point-of-need. For instance, **requiring profile details that aren’t immediately used** (bio, photo, demographics) at signup adds friction for no benefit – better to capture such info when the user engages that feature. Conversely, not asking something critical (like age for a restricted app) early can cause compliance or user experience issues later. **Confidence:** Medium – *Logical deduction from UX studies; success depends on knowing your product’s “must-haves” at entry versus later.*  

## 3. Testing Mobile Apps on Windows – Toolchain & Device Gaps (Failure Modes)

- **Lack of iOS Test Environment on Windows:** **Claim:** A Windows-only developer cannot run an iOS Simulator locally – this often leads to minimal iOS testing prior to launch, a major risk. **Evidence:** Apple’s iOS simulator is part of Xcode (macOS only), so on Windows the only options are using a real iPhone or a cloud service. Many new founders only test on Android or web and ship an iOS build “blind,” which can hide iOS-specific bugs. **Confidence:** High – *Fundamental technical limitation noted in all cross-platform tooling docs.*  

- **Gaps Not Caught in Emulators/Simulators:** **Claim:** Certain features **do not work or are unreliable in emulators**, requiring real device testing before launch. **Evidence:** For example, **push notifications** and **in-app purchase flows** cannot be fully tested on iOS simulators (push notifications historically don’t arrive in simulators). Hardware integrations like camera, GPS, motion sensors, or AR features often behave differently or require physical devices for true validation. Performance issues like memory usage or rendering hitches might not manifest on a powerful PC emulator but will on a mid-range phone. **Confidence:** High – *Common knowledge from mobile dev docs (sensor and push testing usually need hardware).*  

- **Minimum Device Coverage Not Met:** **Claim:** Testing on too narrow a device set leads to launch-day surprises (crashes or layout issues on untested devices). **Evidence:** A solo founder might have only a modern Android phone on hand; if the app isn’t tested on at least one iOS device and an older/slower Android, issues like screen size bugs, OS version incompatibilities, or poor performance could slip through. **Confidence:** Medium – *Anecdotal evidence from many app launch post-mortems where broader device testing would have caught critical bugs.*  

- **Expo/React Native on Windows Networking “Gotchas”:** **Claim:** Developing with Expo or React Native on Windows can hit networking snags that break testing on devices. **Evidence:** By default, Metro’s dev server runs on the PC’s network – if using **WSL2** or VMs, the server may be on a virtual adapter that isn’t accessible to your physical device. Many developers encounter connection errors where the app can’t load bundles from the host PC. Additionally, Windows Firewall can block the dev server port; if the firewall prompt is mis-clicked, local devices or emulators cannot connect to the packager. These issues can stall testing with real devices. **Confidence:** High – *Frequently discussed in developer forums (common configuration issue on Windows).*  

- **Android Emulator & Hyper-V Pitfalls:** **Claim:** Android emulators on Windows can be sluggish or fail to run if virtualization isn’t configured correctly, which new developers often discover the hard way. **Evidence:** Windows 10/11 uses **Hyper-V** or **WHPX** for accelerated emulation. If Hyper-V is disabled (or conversely, if it’s enabled and another hypervisor like HAXM is expected), the Android Virtual Device might run in pure software mode (extremely slow) or not boot at all. This often manifests as inexplicably slow performance or emulator crashes on misconfigured machines. **Confidence:** High – *Known issue documented in Android developer docs and solved by enabling the proper Windows features.*  

- **Reliance on Cloud Build/Device Farms:** **Claim:** Some solo developers use cloud build services (e.g. Expo EAS, Flutter CI) and device farms (BrowserStack, Firebase Test Lab) to cover iOS testing, but over-reliance without local iteration can slow debugging. **Evidence:** While these services provide access to iOS builds on real devices in the cloud, they introduce iteration lag and sometimes surface issues without a straightforward way to debug (e.g. a crash on a cloud device with limited logs). If a founder waits until late-stage to run these, they may uncover critical issues last-minute. **Confidence:** Medium – *Based on typical developer experiences; mitigation requires planning testing cycles with these tools in advance.*  

- **Windows-Specific Dev Environment Traps:** **Claim:** The Windows OS itself can introduce quirks in the mobile development process that cause failures if not addressed. **Evidence:** For instance, connecting an iPhone to a Windows machine for testing is not natively supported (iOS devices can’t be used for live debugging on Windows except via third-party tools). Connecting Android devices requires installing OEM USB drivers – without the correct driver, `adb` won’t recognize the phone. Another example: file path limitations on Windows can break builds (long path issues in Node modules), and case-insensitivity on the filesystem might mask bugs that surface on case-sensitive Linux servers. These platform nuances can lead to “it worked on my PC, why is it broken in production?” scenarios. **Confidence:** Medium – *Mix of documented issues and logical deduction from OS differences (file system, drivers, etc.).*  

---

**In summary,** first-time founders should proactively anticipate these failure modes. By studying past post-mortems and platform guidelines, one can build guardrails against known pitfalls – from avoiding banned growth hacks, to reducing signup friction, to thoroughly testing on the right devices. Each failure mode above represents a branch in a **fault tree** that could harm a launch. Recognizing and mitigating these in advance dramatically improves the odds of a smooth product debut in 2026.
