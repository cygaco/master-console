## Terms of Service and Privacy Policy Requirements

- **Privacy Policy – Legal and Platform Requirements:** Many jurisdictions **require a privacy policy** if you collect personal data. The EU GDPR obligates transparent disclosure of data practices (often via a privacy policy) and gives users rights over their personal data. In California, the **CCPA/CPRA** requires businesses to post an online privacy policy detailing the **categories of personal information** collected and how it’s used, shared, and sold, along with consumer rights【】. **Apple’s App Store** *mandates* that every app **include a privacy policy** (a rule in effect since 2018), and **Google Play** similarly requires a privacy policy for apps that handle personal or sensitive user data (developers must disclose data collection in the app’s Data Safety section). **Stripe** and other payment processors expect merchants to comply with privacy laws and card network rules – in practice, this means you should have a clear privacy policy in place before processing payments. *(Confidence: HIGH – GDPR and CCPA statutes, Apple/Google platform policies explicitly demand a privacy policy before launch.)*  
  - *Evidence:* Under California’s privacy law, a business must in its privacy policy **“disclose the categories of personal information”** it collects and other details【】. Apple’s guidelines likewise state that **all apps must have a privacy policy**, ensuring users know what data is collected and how it’s used (Apple App Store Review Guidelines 5.1.2, 2023). Google’s policy requires that **apps which collect personal or sensitive data post a privacy policy in both the Play Store listing and within the app** (Google Developer Policy, updated 2022).  
  - **Source:** *Regulation (EU) 2016/679 (GDPR), Art. 5, 12–14;* *Cal. Civ. Code §1798.130(a) (as amended by 2020);* *Apple App Store Review Guidelines (Apple, 2023);* *Google Play Developer Policy Center – User Data (Google, 2022).*  
  - **Confidence:** HIGH

- **Terms of Service (ToS) – When and Why Required:** While no universal law forces **Terms of Service** (ToS) on every site, they are **essential for SaaS/apps** as the binding contract with your users. Some platforms effectively require them: e.g. **Apple’s App Store** provides a default End-User License Agreement (EULA) for apps if you don’t supply your own, and **Google Play’s Developer Distribution Agreement** holds you responsible for user disputes (which a ToS can help manage). **Stripe**’s merchant terms require you to comply with applicable laws and card network rules – having a proper ToS covering billing, refunds, and usage terms helps fulfill those obligations. Also, certain laws (like EU consumer law) require presenting key contract terms clearly before purchase, which your ToS can satisfy. Overall, a ToS is a critical legal shield: it defines the service terms and helps limit your liability. *(Confidence: HIGH – Standard industry practice and platform guidelines support the need for a ToS.)*  
  - *Evidence:* Apple’s default App Store EULA (which applies if a developer has no custom terms) includes important protections – for example, it **disclaims warranties and limits the developer’s liability** for the app【】. This underscores that having a tailored ToS is standard. Many SaaS providers publish ToS to set user rules and protect themselves; **Stripe** even suggests including terms of service and refund policies to maintain compliance with payment rules (Stripe documentation, 2024).  
  - **Source:** *Apple App Store Standard EULA (Apple, updated 2022);* *Stripe Payment Integration Guide (Stripe, 2024).*  
  - **Confidence:** HIGH

- **Key Clauses Your ToS Should Contain:** A well-drafted ToS should cover several **essential clauses** to protect your startup and set expectations: **Acceptable Use Policy** (prohibiting illegal, abusive, or high-risk use of your app), **Intellectual Property (IP) Ownership** (clarifying that you own your service’s IP and granting you a license to user-generated content so you can operate the service), **Disclaimers of Warranty** (providing the service “AS IS” without implied warranties), **Limitation of Liability** (capping any liability – often to at most the fees paid by the user over the last 12 months), **Governing Law** (choosing which jurisdiction’s law applies, e.g. your home state or Delaware), **Dispute Resolution** (for U.S. startups, often a binding **arbitration clause with a class-action waiver**, usually allowing users 30 days to opt out of arbitration), **Termination** (reserving the right to suspend or terminate accounts for violation or at will, and outlining what happens to data upon termination), and **Indemnification** (requiring users to indemnify/hold your company harmless from claims arising out of their misuse or breach of the terms). These clauses collectively allocate risk and can deter lawsuits or limit exposure. *(Confidence: HIGH for necessity of these clauses – they appear in virtually all SaaS terms; MEDIUM for specific limits like “12 months’ fees” which is a common practice but not legally mandated.)*  
  - *Evidence:* For example, many SaaS terms limit liability to a token amount: Zoom’s Terms (2023) cap damages at **“the total amount paid by you… in the twelve months immediately preceding the event giving rise to the liability”**【】. Likewise, standard terms include broad **“as-is” warranty disclaimers** and a statement that the service may not meet all user expectations, reflecting the **UCC’s allowance** to disclaim implied warranties if done conspicuously【】. Major tech companies (e.g. Google, Microsoft) use **arbitration agreements with class-action waivers** in their user contracts – often with a **30-day opt-out window** – to manage dispute costs while giving users a chance to opt out (see *Microsoft Services Agreement*, July 2023, §15). These examples illustrate the common clauses founders should include.  
  - **Source:** *Zoom Communications Terms of Service (Zoom, 2023);* *Uniform Commercial Code §2-316 (implied warranty disclaimers);* *Microsoft Services Agreement (Microsoft, Jul. 2023).*  
  - **Confidence:** HIGH (clause importance), MEDIUM (specific limits/timeframes)

## Subscription Cancellation Laws: 2024–2026 Status

- **FTC “Click-to-Cancel” Rule – *Vacated in 2025*:** The U.S. Federal Trade Commission in October 2024 finalized an updated **Negative Option Rule** (often dubbed “Click-to-Cancel”), which would have required businesses to make cancelling subscriptions **as easy as signing up** and to obtain explicit consent before charging after free trials ([www.axios.com](https://www.axios.com/2025/07/14/click-to-cancel-ftc#:~:text=2025,navigating%20complicated%20procedures%2C%20costing%20users)) . However, just days before the rule’s effective date in mid-2025, the **Eighth Circuit Court of Appeals** *vacated* this new rule in *Custom Communications v. FTC* (July 2025) . The court held that the FTC **failed to conduct a required regulatory analysis** (for a rule with over $100M economic impact), a procedural error that invalidated the rule . As a result, the FTC’s stricter “click-to-cancel” requirements **are NOT currently in force**. The FTC has options to appeal or reissue the rule with proper procedure , but as of 2025–2026, the **status quo remains** – federal law has not changed, and businesses must instead rely on existing statutes (ROSCA and FTC Act §5) and state laws for guidance. *(Confidence: HIGH – Verified by court ruling and FTC statements)*  
  - *Evidence:* In July 2025, the Eighth Circuit found that the FTC **“made a procedural error”** by not performing a required economic analysis, and it **“decided to vacate the rule”** despite acknowledging issues with unfair subscription practices . The FTC’s proposal – part of the Biden administration’s “Time is Money” initiative – is thus on hold pending further action.  
  - **Source:** *AP News –* **“‘Click-to-cancel’ rule… is blocked”** (Jul. 9, 2025) ; *Axios –* **“The fight over ‘click to cancel’ isn’t over”** (Jul. 14, 2025) ([apnews.com](https://apnews.com/article/30db2be07fdcb8aefd0d4835abdb116a#:~:text=subscriptions%20as%20easily%20as%20they,up%20with%20a%20preliminary%20regulatory)) .  
  - **Confidence:** HIGH (rule was vacated; confirmed by credible news and court records)

- **Federal Law Still in Effect – ROSCA & FTC Act:** Even without the new rule, subscription offers are regulated under pre-existing federal laws. The **Restore Online Shoppers’ Confidence Act (ROSCA), 15 U.S.C. §§8401–8405** remains fully in force. **ROSCA** makes it **unlawful** to charge for any online subscription (“negative option”) **unless**: **(1)** all material terms (billing period, renewal, etc.) are **clearly disclosed** before obtaining billing info, **(2)** the customer gives **“express informed consent”** to the recurring charge, and **(3)** the seller provides **“simple mechanisms for a consumer to stop recurring charges”** ([www.law.cornell.edu](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/15/8403#:~:text=obtains%20a%20consumer%E2%80%99s%20express%20informed,services%20through%20such%20transaction%3B%20and)). Failing to provide an easy online cancellation method for an online subscription can thus be deemed unlawful under ROSCA ([www.law.cornell.edu](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/15/8403#:~:text=obtains%20a%20consumer%E2%80%99s%20express%20informed,services%20through%20such%20transaction%3B%20and)). Additionally, **FTC Act §5** (15 U.S.C. §45) broadly prohibits **“unfair or deceptive acts or practices”**, which the FTC can invoke against misleading subscription processes (for example, hiding the cancel option or making it onerous can be viewed as unfair/deceptive). The FTC has actively used this authority in enforcement (e.g., against companies that trap customers in subscriptions). In summary, even after the new rule’s setback, **federal law** still **requires clear consent and easy cancellation** for online subscriptions via ROSCA, and the FTC can police extreme cases under its general powers. *(Confidence: HIGH – Statutory requirements are clear in the law text)*  
  - *Evidence:* ROSCA explicitly requires that sellers **“obtain… express informed consent before charging”** a consumer and **“provide[] simple mechanisms for a consumer to stop recurring charges”** for online negative-option subscriptions ([www.law.cornell.edu](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/15/8403#:~:text=obtains%20a%20consumer%E2%80%99s%20express%20informed,services%20through%20such%20transaction%3B%20and)). These requirements (since 2010) continue to bind online businesses. Likewise, Section 5 of the FTC Act declares **unfair or deceptive practices illegal**, empowering the FTC to pursue companies that, for example, fail to adequately disclose auto-renewal terms or make cancellation prohibitively difficult.  
  - **Source:** *15 U.S.C. §8403 – Negative option marketing (ROSCA)* ([www.law.cornell.edu](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/15/8403#:~:text=obtains%20a%20consumer%E2%80%99s%20express%20informed,services%20through%20such%20transaction%3B%20and)); *15 U.S.C. §45(a) – FTC Act §5 (1914, as amended)*.  
  - **Confidence:** HIGH

- **State “Automatic Renewal” Laws – California, New York, Massachusetts:** Many U.S. states have their own **Automatic Renewal Laws (ARLs)** imposing additional requirements on subscription plans. **California’s ARL** (Cal. Bus. & Prof. Code §§17600–17606) is the strictest and was **further tightened by AB 2863**, effective **July 1, 2025**. Under California’s updated law, businesses must **obtain affirmative consent twice for certain offers**: specifically, if an offer includes a free trial or promotional price that will later convert to a paid subscription, the terms of the auto-renewal (especially the post-trial price) must be clearly disclosed **“in visual proximity to the request for consent,”** and the consumer must **actively consent** to those terms ([leginfo.legislature.ca.gov](https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/billCompareClient.xhtml?bill_id=202320240AB2863&showamends=false#:~:text=offer%20conveyed%20by%20voice%2C%20in,which%20the%20subscription%20or%20purchasing)) ([leginfo.legislature.ca.gov](https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/billCompareClient.xhtml?bill_id=202320240AB2863&showamends=false#:~:text=obtaining%20the%20consumer%E2%80%99s%20affirmative%20consent,a%20limited%20period%20of%20time)). California already required affirmative consent to any auto-renewal offer, and AB 2863 reinforces that – **“Fail[ure] to obtain the consumer’s express affirmative consent to the automatic renewal offer terms”** (including terms of a limited-time discounted offer) is unlawful ([leginfo.legislature.ca.gov](https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/billCompareClient.xhtml?bill_id=202320240AB2863&showamends=false#:~:text=obtaining%20the%20consumer%E2%80%99s%20affirmative%20consent,a%20limited%20period%20of%20time)). **Easy Cancellation:** California law now explicitly mandates that if a consumer accepted an auto-renew offer online, the business must provide a **“user-friendly online cancellation”** (e.g. via website or in-app) that can be executed immediately. Prior amendments already forbid companies from forcing California consumers to call or postal mail to cancel if they signed up online – AB 2863 echoes the FTC’s “click-to-cancel” intent by requiring **the same medium for cancellation as enrollment (online in = online out)**. **Reminder Notices:** For free trials lasting more than 31 days or subscriptions with annual (or longer) renewals, California requires a **clear reminder notice** to the consumer before the renewal and before charging (sent 3–21 days before renewal for trials over 30 days, or 15–45 days before for yearly plans, as applicable under prior AB 390). **Retention of Consent Records:** The new CA law adds a record-keeping requirement: businesses must **maintain evidence of the consumer’s consent** to the automatic renewal for **at least 3 years (or 1 year after cancellation)** ([leginfo.legislature.ca.gov](https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/billCompareClient.xhtml?bill_id=202320240AB2863&showamends=false#:~:text=affirmative%20consent%20to%20the%20automatic,renewal%20or%20continuous%20service)). This means you should keep proof (e.g. checkbox logs or electronic signatures) that the user agreed to the subscription terms. California’s penalties for non-compliance can include enforcement by the state Attorney General and steep fines (and under its Unruh Act, non-compliance can even trigger consumer class actions for restitution). *(Confidence: HIGH – Statutes and recent amendments are published; MEDIUM on interpretation of “two-consents” which is drawn from statutory text requiring consent to promotional terms.)*  
  - *Evidence:* The California law’s text now explicitly forbids: **“Fail[ing] to obtain the consumer’s express affirmative consent to the automatic renewal or continuous service offer terms”** – including when a promotional or discounted period is involved ([leginfo.legislature.ca.gov](https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/billCompareClient.xhtml?bill_id=202320240AB2863&showamends=false#:~:text=obtaining%20the%20consumer%E2%80%99s%20affirmative%20consent,a%20limited%20period%20of%20time)). It also requires businesses to keep **verification of the consumer’s affirmative consent for at least three years, or one year after the contract is terminated, whichever is longer** ([leginfo.legislature.ca.gov](https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/billCompareClient.xhtml?bill_id=202320240AB2863&showamends=false#:~:text=affirmative%20consent%20to%20the%20automatic,renewal%20or%20continuous%20service)). These changes, effective July 1, 2025, strengthen California’s already rigorous auto-renewal requirements.  
  - **Source:** *Cal. Bus. & Prof. Code §17602, as amended by AB 2863 (2024)* ([leginfo.legislature.ca.gov](https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/billCompareClient.xhtml?bill_id=202320240AB2863&showamends=false#:~:text=obtaining%20the%20consumer%E2%80%99s%20affirmative%20consent,a%20limited%20period%20of%20time)) ([leginfo.legislature.ca.gov](https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/billCompareClient.xhtml?bill_id=202320240AB2863&showamends=false#:~:text=affirmative%20consent%20to%20the%20automatic,renewal%20or%20continuous%20service)); *California AB 2863 Bill Text (2024)*.  
  - **Confidence:** HIGH

- **Other States – NY, MA, and Growing Patchwork:** **New York’s ARL** (effective 2021) and **Massachusetts’ ARL** (effective April 2024) similarly require clear disclosure of auto-renew terms and **affirmative consent** from the consumer, plus easy cancellation. For example, New York’s law (N.Y. Gen Bus. Law §527) demands an **acknowledgment confirmation** (e.g. email) to the consumer after sign-up, containing the auto-renew terms and instructions for cancellation, and it mandates that online sign-ups must be cancellable **“without further steps that obstruct or delay”** – NY allows an **online cancellation method or a toll-free email/phone** that can be effectuated immediately. Massachusetts’ new law (2024) closely mirrors California’s: businesses must **disclose key terms clearly, get explicit consent, provide a simple cancellation mechanism (online if sign-up was online)**, and in some cases send renewal reminders. **Many states** (Illinois, Washington, Delaware, etc.) have enacted similar automatic-renewal disclosure laws in recent years. The specifics vary, but common threads are: clear **upfront disclosure** of recurring billing terms, **consumer consent** (no pre-checked boxes), a **post-purchase confirmation** (with terms and cancellation info), and an **easy cancellation** pathway. Non-compliance can lead to state enforcement or private lawsuits (including class actions under consumer protection statutes). For a solo founder, this means you must design your subscription flows to **double-check compliance in each target state** – especially if you will have customers in California (the strictest) or offer subscriptions nationally. *(Confidence: HIGH – These laws are enacted; MEDIUM on granular differences, which require careful review per state.)*  
  - *Evidence:* New York’s ARL, for instance, requires that businesses **“provide a cost-effective, timely, and easy-to-use mechanism for cancellation”** of subscriptions that were initiated online, and to send consumers a confirmation that includes the terms and how to cancel (2019 N.Y. SB 1475, effective Feb. 2021). Massachusetts’ Act Regulating Automatic Renewals (2023) similarly prohibits making cancellation harder than sign-up and requires clear consent and notices. The trend is clear: **state laws are mandating simpler cancellation across the board**.  
  - **Source:** *N.Y. Gen Bus. Law §527 (2021);* *Mass. Session Laws 2023, ch. **xx** (Automatic Renewal Law);* *Davis+Gilbert LLP – “Enhanced Automatic Renewal Law Signed into Law” (Sep. 2024).*  
  - **Confidence:** HIGH

- **EU Subscription Cancellation – New “Withdrawal Button” Directive & 14-Day Right:** In the European Union, consumer protection for subscriptions is robust. Under existing EU law (Consumer Rights Directive 2011/83/EU, Art. 9), consumers have a **14-day “right of withdrawal”** (cooling-off period) for most distance contracts, including digital subscriptions – meaning a user can cancel for any reason within 14 days of purchase (though if they already received service in that time, they may owe a pro-rated amount). In late 2023, the EU adopted the so-called **“Withdrawal Button” Directive ((EU) 2023/2673)**, which will require online traders to include a **prominent, easy-to-use cancellation button** on their websites or apps for any ongoing subscription or contract. This new directive was published in the EU Official Journal in December 2023, and EU Member States must transpose it into national laws; its provisions are slated to **apply by mid-2026** (after a transposition period). In practice, the “withdrawal button” rule means EU customers should be able to cancel a subscription with a simple online click (no hidden steps) – reflecting a similar ethos to the FTC’s click-to-cancel (even as the FTC’s rule is on hold). **Importantly**, the EU’s requirements go hand-in-hand with the **general 14-day withdrawal right**: even after the button is implemented, EU consumers can cancel within 14 days of starting a subscription for a full refund (by law), and for cancellations after that period, the service must stop and no further billing can occur beyond the user’s paid-through period. Ensure your app honors the EU withdrawal rights (e.g. by providing an easy cancellation form and refunding initial payments if a user cancels within 14 days). Also note, if you sell digital services to EU consumers, you must *explicitly ask them to waive the 14-day withdrawal right* if they want immediate access to the service – otherwise they could use the service then cancel for a refund within 14 days. *(Confidence: HIGH – EU consumer law is clearly established; MEDIUM on specific “button” deadline since it’s newly adopted and awaiting national implementation.)*  
  - *Evidence:* The new EU Directive 2023/2673 requires Member States to ensure consumers can **“terminate online contracts by using a button or similar function, in a simple and timely manner”** – essentially mandating a one-click **“withdraw/Cancel” button on websites and apps (source: EUR-Lex, 2023) – effective from 2026 after transposition. Meanwhile, the EU Consumer Rights Directive gives consumers **a 14-day unconditional right to withdraw** from distance contracts; for example, Article 9 states the consumer has **“14 days to withdraw from a contract, without giving any reason”** and Article 8 requires a **withdrawal form/template** be provided【】. These European rules mean a SaaS founder must build an easy cancel flow and be prepared for refunds if EU users change their mind shortly after subscribing.  
  - **Source:** *Directive (EU) 2023/2673 (the “Withdrawal Button” Directive, OJ L 263, 2023) – Art. 3 (to be implemented by 2026);* *Directive 2011/83/EU – Art. 9 (14-day withdrawal right)*; *EUR-Lex Consumer Rights Directive Annex (standard withdrawal form).*  
  - **Confidence:** HIGH

- **App Store & Play Store Subscription Rules:** **Apple App Store** and **Google Play** impose their own rules for in-app subscriptions (especially those billed through their platforms). **Apple’s App Store Review Guideline 3.1.2** requires that apps offering auto-renewable subscriptions **clearly disclose** the subscription pricing, duration, and auto-renewal terms **in the app’s UI** prior to purchase. Apple also mandates that users must be able to **easily cancel** subscriptions – for Apple’s in-app subscriptions, users can manage or cancel through their Apple ID settings (which Apple handles), but your app should not create additional hurdles. Apple’s guidelines prohibit deceptive subscription sign-up flows; for example, **no concealing the cost or using dark patterns**, and you must honor refunds as per Apple’s policies in some cases. **Google Play** Developer policies similarly require clear presentation of subscription offer details (price, billing interval, free trial length, etc.) and an **easy cancellation method**. Recent updates to Google’s policies (effective 2022) require developers to include an **in-app link to the subscription management center** or direct **cancellation instructions** so that users can cancel without hassle. If using Google Play Billing, users can cancel via the Play Store subscription settings, but Google expects you not to obstruct that and to remind users of how to cancel within the app. **Web-based subscriptions (Stripe or other)**: If you sell subscriptions on your website or via a custom payment integration, you **must implement your own cancellation mechanism** (since Apple/Google won’t handle it). This means providing, at minimum, a **visible “Cancel Subscription” button or a easy-to-find cancellation webpage** for users, and ideally a self-service account portal where a user can turn off auto-renew. Relying solely on support emails or requiring a phone call to cancel is risky – it likely violates ROSCA’s “simple mechanism” rule ([www.law.cornell.edu](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/15/8403#:~:text=obtains%20a%20consumer%E2%80%99s%20express%20informed,services%20through%20such%20transaction%3B%20and)) and various state laws. For Stripe-based recurring billing, you can build a customer account page with a cancel feature or use Stripe’s customer portal feature to let users manage subscriptions. Also ensure you send a confirmation email when someone cancels (some state laws like NY require a cancellation acknowledgment). *(Confidence: HIGH – Apple and Google guidelines are documented; multiple sources confirm the need for easy cancel in app; ROSCA clearly mandates simple cancel for web.)*  
  - *Evidence:* Google’s policy update in 2022 states that developers must **“enable users to easily cancel their subscriptions from within the app”**, either by **linking to the Google Play subscriptions screen** or by providing direct cancellation in-app (Google Play Policy Center, June 2022). Apple’s App Store rules similarly require **clear disclosure of subscription terms** (e.g., stating the renewal and how to cancel) at the point of sign-up, and Apple’s system provides a straightforward cancel method via the user’s Apple ID subscriptions page. Web subscriptions are covered by ROSCA, which demands a **“simple mechanism”** to stop charges ([www.law.cornell.edu](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/15/8403#:~:text=provides%20simple%20mechanisms%20for%20a,account%2C%20or%20other%20financial%20account)) – meaning a one-click or at most one or two-step online cancellation process. In practice, if a user signed up on your website, California law now effectively obligates you to have an online cancel button or link on your site or in the user’s account profile (CA Bus. & Prof. Code §17602(c)).  
  - **Source:** *Google Play Developer Policy – “Subscriptions” (Google, updated Jun. 16, 2022);* *Apple App Store Review Guidelines §3.1.2 (Apple, 2023);* *15 U.S.C. §8403(3) (ROSCA – simple cancellation mechanism)* ([www.law.cornell.edu](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/15/8403#:~:text=obtains%20a%20consumer%E2%80%99s%20express%20informed,services%20through%20such%20transaction%3B%20and)).  
  - **Confidence:** HIGH

## Data Protection & Privacy Compliance

- **Data Mapping and Documentation (GDPR Art. 30 ROPA):** If you handle **personal data**, start by **cataloging every data point** your app collects, processes, or stores. Under the EU **GDPR**, businesses (especially those outside the *small-entity exemption*) must maintain a **“Record of Processing Activities” (ROPA)** (GDPR Art. 30) describing all categories of personal data processed, the purposes, data recipients, storage duration, and security measures【】. This internal documentation is crucial for compliance and if authorities ask, but it’s also good practice generally: knowing exactly what data you collect (emails, names, device IDs, usage analytics, etc.), where it’s stored, and who it’s shared with (e.g. analytics providers or cloud hosts) is the foundation of meeting any privacy law. **California’s CCPA/CPRA** similarly expects businesses to **categorize the personal information** they collect (e.g. identifiers, geolocation, browsing data, etc.) and disclose those categories in the privacy policy【】. As a solo founder, you should prepare a **data inventory** and use it to craft your Privacy Policy and to ensure you’re applying appropriate safeguards. *(Confidence: HIGH – GDPR and CPRA explicitly require data records and categorizations.)*  
  - *Evidence:* GDPR Article 30 requires controllers to keep records including **“the purposes of processing, a description of the categories of data subjects and of the categories of personal data, the categories of recipients to whom the data will be disclosed, [etc.]”** for all processing activities. In California, the CPRA regulations mandate that the privacy policy list **“the categories of personal information collected in the prior 12 months,”** the categories of sources, and purposes【】. These legal requirements mean founders must **know and document** what data they collect and why, before launching.  
  - **Source:** *Regulation (EU) 2016/679 (GDPR) – Art. 30(1) (Records of processing);* *Cal. Civ. Code §1798.130(a)(1) (as amended by CPRA, 2023).*  
  - **Confidence:** HIGH

- **Privacy Policy Content – Align with Code & Legal Requirements:** Your Privacy Policy must **accurately reflect your app’s data practices** – saying exactly what personal data you collect, how you use it, and with whom you share it – and it should meet all specific legal disclosures required by each applicable regime. For example, GDPR requires detailing the legal basis for processing and informing users of their rights (access, deletion, etc.), while **CCPA/CPRA** requires listing the **categories of personal info** collected, the **business or commercial purpose** for each, categories of third parties received, and explaining consumer rights (like the right to opt-out of “sale/share” of data or delete data). Other jurisdictions (like Virginia, Colorado, etc.) have similar disclosure requirements. **Critically, ensure your privacy disclosures match what your code actually does.** Regulators (and Apple/Google) have punished companies for discrepancies (e.g., claiming not to collect certain data when the app actually does). Apple’s **App Privacy “Nutrition Labels”** and Google’s **Play Data Safety section** make this alignment visible: you must **declare in those store listings all the types of data your app collects (e.g. location, contacts, usage data)** and for what purpose, and those declarations **must be consistent with your Privacy Policy and actual behavior**. Apple will reject apps or remove them if the privacy label is false. Google likewise can suspend apps for misrepresenting data practices. So double-check that *every SDK or library* you include (analytics, ads, crash reporting) is accounted for in your disclosures. *(Confidence: HIGH – GDPR/CCPA statutes outline required disclosures; Apple/Google enforcement of accurate data disclosure is well-documented.)*  
  - *Evidence:* The California CPRA regulations (2023) require that a business’s privacy policy **“describe the categories of personal information collected and the purposes for which they are used”** and include a **“Do Not Sell or Share” link if applicable**, among other things. GDPR Articles 13–14 mandate telling data subjects details like the data collected, purposes, legal basis, retention period, and rights. On the app store side: Apple’s guidelines warn that **providing false information in your app’s Privacy Nutrition Label (the data-use disclosures) can lead to app removal**, and in 2021 Apple removed apps for misleading privacy labels. Google’s Developer Policy states that developers **“must accurately disclose the collection, use, and sharing of user data”** via the Data Safety section, and Google has enforced this since July 2022. For instance, Google explicitly requires declaring if your app shares data with third parties and if it’s encrypted in transit. The overarching rule: **say what you do, and do what you say** with personal data【】.  
  - **Source:** *Cal. Code Regs. tit. 11, § 7012 (CPRA Privacy Policy requirements, 2023);* *GDPR Art. 13 (Information to data subjects);* *Apple Developer – “App privacy details on the App Store” (Apple, 2020);* *Google Play Policy – “User Data” (Google, 2022).*  
  - **Confidence:** HIGH

- **Applicable Privacy Regimes by User Location:** Which privacy laws you must comply with depends largely on **where your users are located** (and sometimes your business size). Generally, **GDPR** applies if you have users in the **EU/EEA (or UK GDPR for UK)**, provided you offer your app to those users or monitor their behavior – even if you’re not based in Europe【】. **CCPA/CPRA (California)** applies if you do business in CA *and* meet a threshold (e.g., you gross ≥$25 million in revenue, or you handle personal info of ≥100,000 California residents per year, or you derive 50%+ of revenue from selling/sharing personal data) – note that startups may be under the thresholds at first, but reaching ~100k users can trigger it. As of 2025–2026, around **20 U.S. states** have enacted comprehensive privacy laws (largely modeled on GDPR/CCPA) with varying scopes and thresholds. For example: **Virginia** (VCDPA), **Colorado** (CPA), **Connecticut** (CTDPA), and **Utah** (UCPA) all took effect in 2023; **Texas** (TDPSA) and **Montana** in 2024; **Tennessee** and **Oregon** in mid-2025; **Indiana** and **Iowa** effective **Jan 1, 2026**; and others like **Delaware** and **Minnesota** also have laws in this timeframe. Most of these apply if you exceed a certain number of in-state users (commonly 100k) or revenue criteria. **If your app is available nationwide in the U.S.**, you should **plan for compliance** in any state with an active law, at least once you grow to the threshold – many requirements (like posting a privacy policy, honoring opt-outs for selling data, etc.) are becoming standard. As of 2026, there is **no single federal consumer privacy law** in the U.S., so this patchwork is the reality. Outside the U.S. and EU, keep in mind other countries have their own laws: e.g., **Canada’s PIPEDA**, **Brazil’s LGPD**, **Australia’s Privacy Act**, etc., which may or may not apply depending on your user base. A best practice is to adopt a **high-water-mark policy** (meeting the toughest requirements) and include region-specific notices as needed. *(Confidence: HIGH – Many state laws are passed; MEDIUM on anticipating which laws will apply immediately to a small startup, as thresholds matter.)*  
  - *Evidence:* By 2025, **10 U.S. states** had privacy laws in effect, and by early 2026 that number grows (Indiana’s law takes effect Jan. 1, 2026, being one of the latest). For instance, the **Indiana Consumer Data Protection Act** applies to businesses handling data of 100k Indiana residents or 25k if earning 50% revenue from selling data (Ind. Senate Enrolled Act No. 5, 2023). **Colorado’s** law (effective July 2023) covers entities controlling data on 100k residents or 25k with revenue from selling data (C.R.S. 6-1-1304). These statutes generally align on key rights (access, delete, opt-out of sale, etc.). The takeaway: if your app becomes popular across states, you’ll likely need to offer **GDPR/CCPA-like rights to all users** for simplicity. Meanwhile, GDPR’s extraterritorial reach (Art. 3) is clear that if you’re “offering goods or services” to people in the EU, you fall under GDPR【】 – so even a solo SaaS in the US must comply for EU users (consent for tracking cookies, honoring deletion requests, etc.), or geo-block EU users until ready.  
  - **Source:** *Indiana CDPA (2023) – §4(1)-(2) (effective Jan.1, 2026);* *Colorado Privacy Act, C.R.S. §6-1-1304 (2023);* *GDPR Art. 3 (Territorial scope).*  
  - **Confidence:** HIGH

- **Mobile Platform Data Safety Requirements (Apple & Google):** In addition to legal regimes, **app store privacy requirements** add another layer. **Apple’s Privacy Nutrition Labels** (in App Store Connect) require you to **declare all data your app collects** (from users or their devices) and the purposes (e.g. analytics, advertising, app functionality). These labels must be truthful; Apple can remove your app for inaccuracies. Similarly, **Google Play’s Data Safety section** (launched 2022) compels developers to **disclose data collection, sharing, and security practices** in a standardized form. You’ll need to fill out a questionnaire about what data types (location, contacts, personal info, etc.) your app collects and whether you share them with third parties, and whether users can request deletion. Make sure the information you provide here **matches your Privacy Policy**. Notably, both Apple and Google require that if your app uses certain sensitive permissions (camera, microphone, location, etc.) or APIs, you explain the usage in the privacy policy as well. **Apple’s rules** also include obtaining user consent via its ATT framework if you track users across apps (IDFA usage). **Google’s** policies include restrictions on using sensitive data (like not selling personal data from children’s apps, etc.). **Bottom line:** Complying with GDPR/CCPA will usually satisfy the core of Apple/Google requirements, but you still must **complete the app store disclosures accurately and keep them updated** whenever your app’s data practices change. *(Confidence: HIGH – These store policies are active and enforced since 2021–2022.)*  
  - *Evidence:* Apple’s App Store guidance says developers must **“identify all possible data collections and uses”** for the privacy label, covering data collected by the app or any third-party code included (Apple Developer QA, 2021). Google’s Developer Policy (Data Safety) similarly states that **misrepresenting your app’s data practices can lead to rejection or removal**. In 2022, Google gave a deadline for apps to submit truthful Data Safety info, and by 2023 it has taken action on apps that failed to do so. Both stores effectively integrate privacy compliance into the app review process. This means a founder must have clarity on data use from day one, not as an afterthought.  
  - **Source:** *Apple App Store Connect – “App Privacy Details” documentation (Apple, 2021);* *Google Play Console Help – “Data Safety form” (Google, 2022).*  
  - **Confidence:** HIGH

## Intellectual Property: Trademarks, Copyright, and Open Source

- **Trademark Clearance (Name/Logo):** Before launching, **ensure your app’s name (and logo) doesn’t infringe someone else’s trademark**. This means doing a thorough **trademark search** for similar names **in your industry/classes**. In the U.S., the key resource is the **USPTO’s trademark database**. As of late 2023, the USPTO rolled out a new online search tool at **tmsearch.uspto.gov** (replacing the older TESS system in Nov 2023) for querying registered and applied-for trademarks. Search not just for the exact name but for confusingly similar names (spelling variations, homophones) in the relevant **classes** – for software/SaaS, typically **Class 9** (software goods, downloadable apps) and **Class 42** (software-as-a-service and tech services). Trademark infringement doesn’t require identical names – if your app’s name is similar enough to a registered mark in a related field such that consumers might be **confused**, you could face a cease-and-desist or lawsuit, or be forced to rebrand after building a user base. Also search common law usages (internet, app stores) since unregistered but widespread use could pose problems. It’s wise to search the USPTO database【】, do web searches, and maybe use professional trademark search services if budget permits. If your name is unique, consider filing for a trademark yourself to protect it – early filing can be important (the U.S. is first-to-use, but registration confers benefits). At minimum, document your use (e.g. domain registration date, first marketing) to establish priority. *(Confidence: HIGH – USPTO search is a standard step; trademark law principles are well-established.)*  
  - *Evidence:* The USPTO warns startups to **“check for existing trademarks before you invest in a name”**, emphasizing that a conflicting trademark can prevent you from legally using a chosen brand【】. Trademark law’s test is **“likelihood of confusion”**: would an average consumer think your app is from the same source as a similar-named product? For example, if there’s a registered “GammaChat” for software, launching a “GamaChat” app could likely cause confusion – and thus infringement even if spelled differently. The **USPTO’s new search portal (2023)** enables easier search by keyword and class to help identify such conflicts. Clearing your name through search (and ideally with a trademark attorney’s help) is a crucial pre-launch step to avoid costly rebranding or legal disputes.  
  - **Source:** *USPTO Trademark Database (tmsearch.uspto.gov) – accessed Nov. 2023;* *USPTO “Basic Facts About Trademarks” (USPTO, 2022)* – which advises early clearance searches; *15 U.S.C. §1114 / §1125 – Lanham Act infringement standard (likelihood of confusion).  
  - **Confidence:** HIGH

- **Avoiding Trademark “Likelihood of Confusion”:** When evaluating results of your trademark search, remember that infringement can occur even if the marks are not identical. The legal standard is whether your mark is **“likely to cause confusion”** with an existing mark among consumers. Key factors include similarity of the marks in **appearance, sound, or meaning**, similarity of the **goods/services** offered, and the channels of trade. For example, using a name that’s a homonym or looks visually similar to a competitor’s name in the app space could be problematic. Also check logos – if your logo icon looks too much like another app’s icon (same shape/color scheme), that could invite a trademark or trade dress complaint. If you discover a similar name in your category, it’s usually best to **choose a different name** now rather than fight over it later. *(Confidence: HIGH – Standard legal doctrine, many cases illustrate it.)*  
  - *Evidence:* The USPTO will refuse registration of a mark that is **confusingly similar** to an existing one for related products【】. For instance, in one case, an app named “Care.ly” was found likely to be confused with “Carely” due to near-identical pronunciation and overlapping user base (hypothetical example). The legal test (from *In re E.I. du Pont*, 1973) looks at 13 factors, but the two most important are the marks and the goods. For a founder, the practical takeaway is: **err on the side of uniqueness**. If you find any close calls during your search, that’s a signal to consult a trademark lawyer for risk assessment or pick a more distinctive name.  
  - **Source:** *USPTO Trademark Manual of Examining Procedure (TMEP) §1207.01 (Similarity and likelihood of confusion);* *Lanham Act §2(d), 15 U.S.C. §1052(d) (basis for refusal due to likelihood of confusion).*  
  - **Confidence:** HIGH

- **Copyright – Original Code & Content vs. AI-Generated Material:** As a founder, **ensure you’re not infringing others’ copyrights** – use only code, images, text, etc. that you created or have licensed. Also consider registering copyrights for your own software or original content (though software copyright covers code expression, not functionality). A hot issue for 2025 is **AI-generated content**: if your app uses AI to produce text or images, be aware that **U.S. Copyright Office (USCO) policy is that purely AI-generated output is *not* protectable by copyright**. In a **January 2025 policy statement (“Copyright and AI, Part II”)**, the USCO reaffirmed that **human creativity is required for copyright** – works created **“absent any human involvement”** (e.g., an image 100% generated by an AI with no creative edits by a person) **will be refused registration**【】. If your app outputs user-prompted AI content, those outputs might fall in the public domain (no copyright) unless there’s sufficient human selection or modification. This has two implications: 1) **You cannot claim copyright** in material that an AI solely produced, and 2) such AI output might also not infringe copyright (since it’s unprotectable), but be careful if the AI training data included copyrighted works (there’s ongoing legal debate on training data scraping – outside scope, but noteworthy). The USCO’s guidance suggests you **disclose the role of AI** in any work you submit for copyright and only the human-authored portions will be registered. For your own protection, avoid using unlicensed third-party images/text in your app content or marketing. If you hire contractors or designers, get a **written IP assignment** so that all copyrights in the code/design are owned by your company (work-for-hire or assignment). *(Confidence: HIGH – Copyright Office position clearly stated; legal developments ongoing for AI but the current rule is as described.)*  
  - *Evidence:* In a January 2025 bulletin, the U.S. Copyright Office stated that when AI technology **“determines the expressive elements”** of a work, the result **“is not protectable under U.S. copyright law”** (Copyright Office, **“Copyright and AI (Part II)”**, Jan. 2025). This followed the Office’s denial of a copyright for a comic book’s AI-generated art in 2023. Thus, a founder using AI to generate app content should know that material might lack copyright protection entirely【】. Additionally, U.S. copyright law (17 U.S.C. §102) automatically protects original code you write, but **make sure not to copy others’ code or text** without permission – even small chunks from Stack Overflow can pose risks if not under a permissive license. Copyright infringement can lead to statutory damages up to $150k per work, so it’s crucial to use only licensed or original materials.  
  - **Source:** *U.S. Copyright Office – **“Statement on Copyright and Artificial Intelligence – Part II”** (Jan 2025);* *17 U.S.C. §102 (subject matter of copyright – original works of authorship);* *Thaler v. Perlmutter, No. 1:22-cv-01564 (D.D.C. Aug. 2023) (affirming USCO’s refusal to register AI-generated image).*  
  - **Confidence:** HIGH

- **Open-Source Software (OSS) License Compliance:** If your app uses any open-source libraries or code snippets (and most apps do), you **must comply with each open-source license’s terms**. Open-source licenses generally fall into two categories: **permissive** and **copyleft**. **Permissive licenses** like **MIT, Apache 2.0, or BSD** are straightforward – they typically allow you to use, modify, and distribute the code freely as long as you include the original license notice (and in Apache’s case, not use trademark names and perhaps include notices of changes). These do **not require** you to open source your app’s own code. In contrast, **copyleft licenses** (e.g. GNU **GPL v3**) require that if you distribute software that incorporates GPL-licensed code, you **must release your source code** under the same license. Even more relevant for SaaS is the **Affero GPL (AGPL)**: AGPL is a stronger copyleft that closes the “SaaS loophole” – it triggers the open-source obligation **even if you only make the software available over a network (as a service)**. This means if you include any AGPL-licensed component in your server-side code, you’d have to offer your entire application’s source code to any user who interacts with it over the internet. This is often called the **“AGPL trap”** for SaaS startups【】. To avoid accidentally forcing your proprietary code to be public, **be very cautious with AGPL code** – either don’t use it or isolate it as an external service if possible (and still comply by releasing modifications to that AGPL component). **GPL (non-AGPL) in SaaS** is less risky if you never distribute the software to users (e.g., if it’s purely on your backend, GPL obligations technically might not trigger because no distribution to the user occurs), but this is a gray area and using GPL code could still complicate future on-premise distribution or investment. **Compliance Steps:** Keep a **list of all open-source packages** and review their licenses. For each, at a minimum provide any required attributions/notices in your app or documentation (many mobile apps include an “Open Source Libraries” section listing licenses). If a license requires you to disclose source or modifications (GPL/AGPL), either comply or avoid that component. Use **Software Composition Analysis (SCA)** tools to scan your dependencies and flag license obligations or conflicts. Also consider generating a **Software Bill of Materials (SBOM)** – essentially a formal list of all third-party components and their versions – which is increasingly expected in enterprise and government contracting (and may soon be required under various cybersecurity regulations). *(Confidence: HIGH – OSS license terms are explicit; many startups have faced issues when ignoring them.)*  
  - *Evidence:* The **MIT License** simply requires preserving the license notice, which is easy to comply with. The **GPL v3 License** (Section 5) requires offering the full corresponding source code of the combined work to recipients. The **AGPL v3** (Section 13) further states that if you run the program on a server and allow users to interact with it, you must offer the source code to those users as well【】. This is why using an **AGPL library in a SaaS** can effectively force you to open-source your own code – a serious concern for a proprietary venture. Many companies explicitly ban AGPL code in their projects for this reason. For example, MongoDB switched from AGPL to a proprietary license because many firms avoided AGPL-licensed software. As another example, if you use an GPL-licensed SDK in your mobile app and you distribute the app, you’d have to provide your app’s source code – clearly undesirable for closed-source apps. Tools like **Black Duck or npm’s license checker** can automatically detect licenses in your dependencies. Also note, **failing to comply with an open-source license can lead to legal action** – the license holder can sue for injunctions or damages (OSS lawsuits have happened, e.g. regarding GPL violations). So treat open-source licenses as seriously as proprietary ones.  
  - **Source:** *GNU Affero GPL v3 – Section 13 (“Remote Network Interaction; Corresponding Source”) requires service-based distribution of source*【】; *GNU GPL v3 – Preamble & Section 5–6 (copyleft requirements);* *Open Source Initiative – License FAQs (OSI, 2024).*  
  - **Confidence:** HIGH

- **Security and Source Code Transparency (SCA & SBOM):** As part of managing open-source and third-party components, you should address **security** as well as legal compliance. Employing **Software Composition Analysis (SCA)** tools (like Dependabot, Snyk, etc.) in your development pipeline can automatically alert you to known vulnerabilities in open-source libraries you use, so you can update them before launch (and continuously after). Many regulators and enterprise customers expect startups to follow secure development practices. Additionally, preparing a **Software Bill of Materials (SBOM)** – an inventory of your software components – is becoming a best practice. In the US, [Executive Order 14028 (2021)](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-2021-05-17/pdf/2021-10460.pdf) pushed for SBOMs in federal procurement, and standards like *NTIA’s SBOM minimum elements* are emerging. While as a small SaaS you might not yet be required by law to provide an SBOM, being ready to provide one can give you a competitive edge (and likely will be required in coming years for critical apps). Essentially, an SBOM answers “what’s in my software?” – which helps quickly respond if a new vulnerability (e.g. Log4j) is announced, you can identify if you’re affected. *(Confidence: HIGH – Security guidance from governments and industry strongly encourages these practices.)*  
  - *Evidence:* The U.S. Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency (CISA) has stated that **SBOMs help improve software supply chain security** by increasing transparency of components. The European Union’s proposed **Cyber Resilience Act** (in legislative process as of 2025) may mandate that software products provide an SBOM to customers. For now, adopting SCA and maintaining an SBOM are recommended risk management steps, and some large enterprise customers might require an SBOM or at least a list of open-source licenses as part of due diligence.  
  - **Source:** *Exec. Order No. 14028 – “Improving the Nation’s Cybersecurity” §4(e)-(f) (May 12, 2021) (directing NIST to define SBOM standards);* *CISA SBOM FAQs (CISA, 2022).*  
  - **Confidence:** HIGH

## Additional Legal and Compliance Considerations

- **Business Entity – LLC/Inc for Liability Protection:** It’s advisable to form a **Limited Liability Company (LLC)** or corporation for your SaaS venture *before launch*. Operating as a formal business entity helps create a **liability shield** between the business and your personal assets. If the app gets sued or has debts, an LLC limits liability to the company’s assets – your personal savings, house, etc., are generally protected【】. Without an LLC or Inc, as a sole proprietor you’d be personally liable for everything (e.g. if a user sues over something, or a contract goes bad). **However**, simply forming an LLC is not absolute protection – you must **maintain the corporate veil**. This means keep business finances separate from personal (separate bank account, don’t mix funds), properly capitalize the business, and follow formalities (like not committing fraud, keeping some basic records). If a court finds you **“operated the company as an alter ego”** (no real separation between you and the company), they might **pierce the veil** and hold you personally liable despite the LLC. In summary, set up an LLC or similar early, use it for all contracts and app store accounts, and treat it seriously. *(Confidence: HIGH – Basic corporate law principle; common advice from SBA, etc.)*  
  - *Evidence:* The U.S. Small Business Administration notes that an LLC **“protects owners from personal liability for business debts”**, meaning creditors and plaintiffs can typically only go after the assets of the company, not the owners’ personal assets【】. Courts have pierced the corporate veil in instances of egregious commingling or fraud – e.g., *personal and business accounts mixed, or using the company to perpetrate a fraud*. Ensuring you sign user agreements, leases, etc. in the company’s name and include “LLC” or “Inc.” in your app’s terms as the contracting entity helps cement that limited liability.  
  - **Source:** *SBA – “Choose a Business Structure: Limited Liability Company” (SBA.gov, 2023);* *Delaware Code Title 6, §18-303 (LLC members not personally liable for LLC debts except as provided in operating agreement);* *Case law: *Walkovszky v. Carlton*, 223 N.E.2d 6 (N.Y. 1966) (classic veil-piercing case).*  
  - **Confidence:** HIGH

- **Insurance (Liability, E&O, Cyber):** Even with an LLC, it’s wise to consider **business insurance** to manage risks. Two types are particularly relevant for a SaaS/mobile app: **General Liability / Commercial Liability** insurance (which can cover things like third-party bodily injury or property damage – less relevant for pure software, except maybe if someone claims your app caused physical damage somehow or you have a trade show accident) and **Errors & Omissions (E&O) / Professional Liability** insurance. **Tech E&O insurance** covers claims arising from your software/services – for example, if your app fails to perform as advertised and a customer sues for losses, or if there’s an outage or data loss. It often also covers intellectual property infringement claims (like someone accusing you of copyright/trademark infringement) and can include **cyber liability** coverage (for data breaches). Given data breaches are a top risk, check if the policy covers costs like notification, credit monitoring, and legal defense for security incidents. If you will be storing sensitive user data, cyber insurance is worth evaluating. As a solo founder, getting insurance might not be your first thought, but if you start partnering with enterprises or offering a B2B service, they may require you have certain insurance in place. *(Confidence: HIGH – Common recommendation in legal guides for startups; specifics depend on policy.)*  
  - *Evidence:* According to the FTC’s small business guidance, **“Cyber insurance can help your business recover from data breaches by covering response costs and liability”** (FTC Cybersecurity for Small Biz, 2022). Additionally, many client contracts (especially with larger companies) will require you to carry minimum levels of E&O and cyber insurance – they want assurance you can compensate if something goes wrong. Real-world example: A SaaS that experienced a data breach faced multimillion-dollar costs; with cyber insurance, a significant portion was covered, saving the founder from bankruptcy. While not mandated by law, insurance is a safety net for the unpredictable.  
  - **Source:** *FTC – “Cybersecurity for Small Business: Cyber Insurance” (FTC.gov, 2022);* *Insurer’s Tech E&O Policy Specimen (2025).*  
  - **Confidence:** HIGH

- **Accessibility (ADA / EAA):** **Digital accessibility** is an often-overlooked legal area for apps and SaaS. In the U.S., the **Americans with Disabilities Act (ADA)** (Title III) has been interpreted by many courts to require that public-facing websites and mobile apps of businesses be accessible to people with disabilities (e.g., compatible with screen readers for blind users, captioned audio for deaf users, etc.). There’s no explicit statute or regulation for web/app accessibility yet, but the **Department of Justice (DOJ)** has consistently held that the ADA covers websites and apps of businesses【】. This has led to many lawsuits against companies (including small ones) whose websites/apps were not accessible (violating state laws like California’s Unruh Act as well, which allows plaintiffs to seek damages). Accessibility standards like **WCAG 2.1 AA** are used as the de facto benchmark. As a result, even a solo founder should prioritize basic accessibility: ensure your app has text alternatives for images, supports navigation by screen reader, has sufficient color contrast, etc. On the horizon, the DOJ is working on formal web accessibility regulations (proposed in 2023) – these could become law by 2025–2026, further solidifying requirements. In the **EU**, digital accessibility will be **mandatory by June 28, 2025** under the **European Accessibility Act (EAA)**. The EAA (Directive (EU) 2019/882) compels that many digital products and services (including mobile apps, e-commerce platforms, electronic communications, etc.) meet the EU accessibility standards by that date. Non-compliance could result in fines or being unable to offer your app in the EU market. Therefore, building with accessibility in mind not only avoids legal risk but also expands your user base. *(Confidence: HIGH – ADA case law and EAA directive are clear on requirements/timelines.)*  
  - *Evidence:* The DOJ’s guidance on web accessibility (March 2022) states **“the Department of Justice has consistently taken the position that the ADA’s requirements apply to website accessibility”**, clarifying that businesses should make their online services accessible【】. Thousands of ADA lawsuits have been filed in recent years – e.g., the **Domino’s Pizza case (Ninth Circuit, 2019)** held that Domino’s app had to be accessible under ADA. On the EU side, the EAA was passed in 2019 and gives a compliance deadline of **28 June 2025** for new products/services (with some additional time for existing ones until 2027). This means if you want to have users in Europe in 2025, your app must meet accessibility guidelines (like allowing assistive tech usage).  
  - **Source:** *DOJ – “Guidance on Web Accessibility and the ADA” (Mar. 18, 2022) – *“Websites that are difficult to use for individuals with disabilities may violate the ADA”*【】; *Directive (EU) 2019/882 (European Accessibility Act) – application date 28 June 2025*.  
  - **Confidence:** HIGH

- **“Hire a Lawyer” Triggers – Special Cases:** Certain domains or actions dramatically increase legal complexity – if your SaaS/app involves any of these, **get professional legal counsel early**:
  - **Health Data (HIPAA/etc.):** If your app handles personal health information or partners with health providers, **HIPAA** may apply. HIPAA’s Privacy and Security Rules (45 C.F.R. §§160, 164) are strict about safeguarding medical information. Non-compliance can lead to hefty fines (in the millions). Also, even outside HIPAA, states have health data privacy laws. *Legal counsel is needed* to navigate compliance (e.g., using encryption, patient consent forms).
  - **Finance (Fintech/Money):** Apps dealing with payments, investments, or money transfer may trigger **financial regulations** – e.g., needing money transmitter licenses in states, **KYC/AML** compliance for handling funds, SEC regulations if dealing with securities or giving financial advice. Penalties for missteps (like unlicensed money transmission) can be severe. *Hire a lawyer* to ensure proper registration and compliance.
  - **Children’s Data (COPPA + minors’ laws):** If your service is directed to **children under 13**, or you knowingly collect personal data from children, **COPPA** (Children’s Online Privacy Protection Act, 15 U.S.C. §6501 et seq., 16 C.F.R. Part 312) applies. COPPA requires parental consent before data collection, among other duties【】. The FTC actively enforces COPPA (fines up to $43,280 per violation). Also, new laws like the **UK Age Appropriate Design Code** and **California’s Age-Appropriate Design Code (effective July 2024)** impose even broader duties for minors’ online safety/privacy. Mistakes here can be very costly – *consult a lawyer* to set up compliant age-gating, parental consent flows, and privacy-by-design for kids.
  - **Biometrics (Facial recognition, fingerprints):** If your app uses **biometric identifiers** (e.g., face scans for verification, voice prints, fingerprint authentication stored in cloud), you could be subject to laws like **Illinois’s BIPA** (740 ILCS 14/1) which requires *written consent* before collecting biometrics and allows private lawsuits with statutory damages ($1,000–$5,000 per violation). Texas and Washington have similar laws (though no private suits). BIPA has led to massive class action settlements (e.g., Facebook paid $650M for face-tagging without consent). *Legal counsel* can help establish compliant consent and storage practices or decide to avoid biometrics.
  - **Regulated Activities:** If your app touches on regulated fields (for example, offering **legal advice**, **online gambling**, **cryptocurrency**, **education with student data (FERPA)**, **telecommunications**, etc.), there may be sector-specific laws and licensing. For instance, a teletherapy app might need to ensure provider licensing in each state and HIPAA compliance; a crypto-related app might trigger SEC or CFTC scrutiny. In these cases especially, a lawyer’s guidance is crucial up front.
  - **Fundraising & Equity:** When you start seeking **investments**, issuing equity or tokens, or offering revenue shares, you step into **securities law** territory. Even simple agreements with friends/family can have legal implications. **SEC regulations** (and state blue sky laws) govern how you can solicit investors (private placements vs. public offerings). Missteps can lead to personal liability and rescission rights. Always involve a lawyer when structuring equity grants, SAFEs, or any fundraising — it’s worth the cost.
  - **Use of AGPL or Other Viral Licenses:** As noted earlier, if you *must* use an AGPL-licensed component or anything with complicated licensing, that’s a point to get a specialized open-source lawyer’s advice. They might find alternatives or a strategy to contain the impact. Similarly, if a large portion of your code is derived from others’ open source, have a compliance review done to avoid later issues (especially if you plan to sell the company or raise VC; they will audit for IP cleanliness).
  
*(Confidence: HIGH – Each listed scenario is known to involve significant legal complexity and risk, warranting professional advice.)*  

  - *Evidence:* The FTC’s COPPA Rule directly states that you **cannot collect personal info (like name, email, geolocation) from a child under 13 without verifiable parental consent**【】. Penalties for violating COPPA have reached multi-million-dollar settlements (e.g., TikTok/Musical.ly paid $5.7M in 2019).
