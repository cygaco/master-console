# Apple App Store Approval Requirements (2025–2026) and Common Rejection Reasons

## Privacy Requirements (Guideline 5.1.1 & 5.1.2)

- **Privacy Policy (Guideline 5.1.1(i)) – [Apple Rule]:** Apple **requires** every app to have a Privacy Policy. This must be provided in two places: **(1)** a URL in the App Store Connect listing metadata, and **(2)** an easily accessible section within the app itself ([developer.apple.com](https://developer.apple.com/appstore/resources/approval/guidelines.html#:~:text=,policy%20must%20clearly%20and%20explicitly)). The policy must clearly explain what user data the app collects, how it's used, etc. This is not just a best practice but a strict App Store rule – apps *without* a Privacy Policy or with a missing link **will be rejected** (Guideline 5.1.1(i)).  
  **Evidence:** *Apple’s App Store Review Guideline 5.1.1(i) states, “All apps must include a link to their privacy policy in the App Store Connect metadata field and within the app in an easily accessible manner.”* ([developer.apple.com](https://developer.apple.com/appstore/resources/approval/guidelines.html#:~:text=,policy%20must%20clearly%20and%20explicitly))  
  **Confidence:** HIGH  
  **Source:** App Store Review Guidelines (Apple, updated Nov 13, 2025)

- **In-App Account Deletion (Guideline 5.1.1(v)) – [Apple Rule]:** If your app allows account creation, Apple **requires** that it also offers a way for users to **delete their account** from within the app ([developer.apple.com](https://developer.apple.com/appstore/resources/approval/guidelines.html#:~:text=%2A%20%28v%29%20Account%20Sign,The)). This rule has been in effect since 2022, reflecting Apple’s privacy stance that users should have control over their data. Failing to provide in-app account deletion for apps with account signup will lead to rejection under Guideline 5.1.1(v).  
  **Evidence:** *Guideline 5.1.1(v) says: “If your app supports account creation, you must also offer account deletion within the app.”* ([developer.apple.com](https://developer.apple.com/appstore/resources/approval/guidelines.html#:~:text=%2A%20%28v%29%20Account%20Sign,The))  
  **Confidence:** HIGH  
  **Source:** App Store Review Guidelines (Apple, updated Nov 13, 2025)

- **App Privacy “Nutrition Label” – [Apple Rule]:** Apple requires developers to fill out the **App Privacy questionnaire** in App Store Connect for each app. This is the privacy “nutrition label” displayed on the App Store, detailing what data the app collects and how it’s used. **Providing this information is mandatory** for submitting new apps or app updates ([developer.apple.com](https://developer.apple.com/app-store/app-privacy-details/#:~:text=can%20learn%20about%20some%20of,and%20app%C2%A0updates%20to%20the%20App%C2%A0Store)). The questionnaire covers data collected by the app and any third-party SDKs it uses. Submissions without a completed (and accurate) privacy questionnaire will be rejected or removed for violating Apple’s **App Store Privacy mandate** (Guideline 5.1.1).  
  **Evidence:** *Apple’s documentation states that developers “need to provide information about your app’s privacy practices… in App Store Connect. This information is **required** to submit new apps and app updates to the App Store.”* ([developer.apple.com](https://developer.apple.com/app-store/app-privacy-details/#:~:text=can%20learn%20about%20some%20of,and%20app%C2%A0updates%20to%20the%20App%C2%A0Store))  
  **Confidence:** HIGH  
  **Source:** Apple Developer – *App privacy details on the App Store* (Apple, updated 2023)

- **Privacy Manifest & Required Reason APIs (effective May 1, 2024) – [Apple Rule]:** Apple introduced **Privacy Manifest** files (`PrivacyInfo.xcprivacy`) and a list of **Required Reason APIs** to increase transparency and user privacy ([developer.apple.com](https://developer.apple.com/news/?id=3d8a9yyh#:~:text=Developers%20are%20responsible%20for%20all,additional%20privacy%20protection%20for%20users)) ([developer.apple.com](https://developer.apple.com/news/?id=3d8a9yyh#:~:text=Starting%20May%201%3A%20You%E2%80%99ll%20need,added%20as%20a%20binary%20dependency)). Certain sensitive APIs (that could be misused for fingerprinting or data access) now require the developer to declare an “approved reason” for usage in the app’s privacy manifest. Starting **May 1, 2024**, any new app or update that uses these APIs **must include** the approved usage reasons in the privacy manifest, or App Store Connect will **reject the build** ([developer.apple.com](https://developer.apple.com/news/?id=3d8a9yyh#:~:text=Starting%20May%201%3A%20You%E2%80%99ll%20need,added%20as%20a%20binary%20dependency)). (Apple began warning devs of missing reasons from March 2024, and enforcement began May 1.) This requirement is **Apple-mandated** and enforced in the review process – not just a guideline but a submission blocker.  
  **Evidence:** *Apple’s February 29, 2024 news bulletin: “Starting May 1: You’ll need to include approved reasons for the listed APIs used by your app’s code to upload a new or updated app to App Store Connect.”* ([developer.apple.com](https://developer.apple.com/news/?id=3d8a9yyh#:~:text=Starting%20May%201%3A%20You%E2%80%99ll%20need,SDK%20that%20includes%20its%20privacy))  
  **Confidence:** HIGH  
  **Source:** Apple Developer News – *Privacy updates for App Store submissions* (Feb 29, 2024)

- **“Third-Party AI” Data Sharing Consent (Guideline 5.1.2(i), added Nov 2025) – [Apple Rule]:** In response to the rise of AI services, Apple updated its guidelines in late 2025 to **clamp down on sharing user data with AI**. Guideline 5.1.2(i) now explicitly requires that apps **disclose** to users if their personal data will be shared with third parties **including any third-party AI services** – and to obtain the user’s **explicit consent** before doing so ([developer.apple.com](https://developer.apple.com/appstore/resources/approval/guidelines.html#:~:text=,Learn%20more%20about)) ([developer.apple.com](https://developer.apple.com/news/?id=ey6d8onl#:~:text=,explicit%20permission%20before%20doing%20so)). In practice, if your app sends user data to an external AI API or model (for example, to an AI analytics or generative service), you must inform the user and get opt-in permission first. This is an Apple rule as of November 13, 2025 – apps in violation (sharing data with AI without consent) can be **rejected under 5.1.2(i)**.  
  **Evidence:** *Apple’s guideline update notes (Nov 13, 2025) state: “5.1.2(i): Clarifies that you must clearly disclose where personal data will be shared with third parties, **including with third-party AI**, and obtain explicit permission before doing so.”* ([developer.apple.com](https://developer.apple.com/news/?id=ey6d8onl#:~:text=,explicit%20permission%20before%20doing%20so))  
  **Confidence:** HIGH  
  **Source:** Apple Developer News – *Updated App Review Guidelines now available* (Nov 13, 2025)

> **Note:** All the above privacy-related items are **required by Apple** policy, not just best practices. A non-compliant app (missing privacy policy, lacking account deletion, inaccurate privacy info, etc.) will **not pass App Review** until these are fixed.

## App Completeness & Minimum Functionality

- **“Landing Page” or Waitlist Apps – [Launch Best-Practice, *not* Apple requirement]:** Some founders create a simple “coming soon” app or waitlist sign-up to gauge demand, but **Apple does not require or encourage this** for App Store launch. In fact, submitting an app that is essentially a placeholder (with just a landing page, sign-up form, or waitlist) is likely to be **rejected** by Apple. This isn’t an Apple rule to *have* a waitlist – it’s a startup strategy. From Apple’s perspective, such an app often **violates guidelines** on completeness and content: it may be seen as having *“placeholder text, empty content”* or as *“primarily marketing material”*, which Apple explicitly disallows ([developer.apple.com](https://developer.apple.com/appstore/resources/approval/guidelines.html#:~:text=available%20for%20pre,app%20bundles%20and%20binaries%20that)) ([developer.apple.com](https://developer.apple.com/appstore/resources/approval/guidelines.html#:~:text=animation%20is%20not%20enough.%20,the%20download%20and%20prompt%20users)). In short, **demand validation is a business best-practice, not an App Store requirement** – and if done, it should be outside the App Store or supplemented by real app functionality.  
  **Evidence:** *Apple’s Guideline 2.1(a) warns that “placeholder text, empty websites, and other temporary content should be scrubbed before submission,” and that apps should be final, with all features in place ([developer.apple.com](https://developer.apple.com/appstore/resources/approval/guidelines.html#:~:text=available%20for%20pre,app%20bundles%20and%20binaries%20that)). Likewise, Guideline 4.2.2 states that apps “shouldn’t primarily be marketing materials, advertisements, web clippings, or a collection of links.”* ([developer.apple.com](https://developer.apple.com/appstore/resources/approval/guidelines.html#:~:text=animation%20is%20not%20enough.%20,the%20download%20and%20prompt%20users)) These rules show that a barebones landing-page app would be non-compliant.  
  **Confidence:** HIGH  
  **Source:** App Store Review Guidelines (Apple, updated Nov 13, 2025)

- **App Completeness (Guideline 2.1) – [Apple Rule]:** Apple requires that your app is **complete and fully functional** upon submission – not a beta, demo, or wireframe. Per Guideline 2.1, all app features, metadata, and links must be working; *“Submissions to App Review… should be final versions with all necessary metadata and fully functional URLs included”*. Any placeholder content (like “Lorem ipsum” text or “coming soon” screens) must be removed ([developer.apple.com](https://developer.apple.com/appstore/resources/approval/guidelines.html#:~:text=available%20for%20pre,app%20bundles%20and%20binaries%20that)). If login is required to access the app, you **must provide valid demo credentials** for the reviewers, or enable a demo mode for Apple  ([developer.apple.com](https://developer.apple.com/appstore/resources/approval/guidelines.html#:~:text=temporary%20content%20should%20be%20scrubbed,exhibits%20your%20app%E2%80%99s%20full%20features)). In practice, this means a solo founder should ensure the app has its core features implemented and no obvious “under construction” sections. Apple will **reject** apps that are incomplete, crash, or have broken features (common **Guideline 2.1** rejection).  
  **Evidence:** *Guideline 2.1(a): “...apps you make available for pre-order should be final versions... placeholder text, empty websites, and other temporary content should be scrubbed before submission. Make sure your app has been tested on-device for bugs and stability... and include demo account info (and turn on your back-end service!) if your app includes a login.”* ([developer.apple.com](https://developer.apple.com/appstore/resources/approval/guidelines.html#:~:text=available%20for%20pre,app%20bundles%20and%20binaries%20that)) This underscores that the app must be complete, with no placeholders, and with test login provided if needed.  
  **Confidence:** HIGH  
  **Source:** App Store Review Guidelines (Apple, updated Nov 13, 2025)

- **Minimum Functionality (Guideline 4.2) – [Apple Rule]:** Apple rejects apps that do not provide sufficient utility, content, or unique value. As a solo founder, ensure your app is not just a **“thin wrapper”** around a website or a generic template with minimal customization. Guideline 4.2 requires that apps feel “app-like” and not merely be web content or marketing material in an app container ([developer.apple.com](https://developer.apple.com/appstore/resources/approval/guidelines.html#:~:text=Your%20app%20should%20include%20features%2C,be%20submitted%20to%20the%20Apple%C2%A0Books%C2%A0Store)) ([developer.apple.com](https://developer.apple.com/appstore/resources/approval/guidelines.html#:~:text=animation%20is%20not%20enough.%20,the%20download%20and%20prompt%20users)). For example, an app that just opens a mobile website with little native functionality, or a template-based app with nothing unique, can be rejected under **4.2.0** (“Minimum Functionality”). Apple specifically says *“Your app should include features, content, and UI that elevate it beyond a repackaged website… If your app is not particularly useful, unique, or ‘app-like,’ it doesn’t belong on the App Store.”* ([developer.apple.com](https://developer.apple.com/appstore/resources/approval/guidelines.html#:~:text=Your%20app%20should%20include%20features%2C,be%20submitted%20to%20the%20Apple%C2%A0Books%C2%A0Store)) Also, **Guideline 4.2.2** forbids apps that are primarily marketing or aggregations of links ([developer.apple.com](https://developer.apple.com/appstore/resources/approval/guidelines.html#:~:text=animation%20is%20not%20enough.%20,the%20download%20and%20prompt%20users)). In short, you need a **meaningful, stand-alone app**.  
  **Evidence:** *Extract from Guideline 4.2: “Your app should include features, content, and UI that elevate it beyond a repackaged website. If your app is not particularly useful, unique, or ‘app-like,’ it doesn’t belong on the App Store.” Additionally, “Other than catalogs, apps shouldn’t primarily be marketing materials... or a collection of links.”* ([developer.apple.com](https://developer.apple.com/appstore/resources/approval/guidelines.html#:~:text=Your%20app%20should%20include%20features%2C,be%20submitted%20to%20the%20Apple%C2%A0Books%C2%A0Store)) ([developer.apple.com](https://developer.apple.com/appstore/resources/approval/guidelines.html#:~:text=animation%20is%20not%20enough.%20,the%20download%20and%20prompt%20users))  
  **Confidence:** HIGH  
  **Source:** App Store Review Guidelines (Apple, updated Nov 13, 2025)

> **Note:** The idea of a minimal app just to test user interest may be a good **product strategy**, but Apple’s rules demand that anything on the App Store be a **complete, useful app**. If you want to do a waitlist or landing page, it's best done via a website or TestFlight (closed beta) rather than a public App Store release, to avoid rejection.

## Beta Testing (TestFlight) and Account Login Rules

- **TestFlight Internal vs External Testing – [Apple Process]:** *TestFlight* is Apple’s beta distribution platform, which a solo founder can use to test the app with users before full release. **Internal testing** (up to 100 members of your App Store Connect team) can start immediately once you upload a build – no App Review needed for internal testers. In contrast, **External testing** (inviting outside beta users, up to 10,000 testers) **requires a Beta App Review** by Apple. This means when you’re ready to send your app to external testers (even via TestFlight), you must provide Beta App Review information (like a description of what to test) and wait for Apple to approve the beta build. The Beta review is generally quicker and somewhat more lenient than full review, but **most App Store guidelines still apply** (e.g. obvious guideline violations will cause the beta to be rejected). The distinction is: *Internal TestFlight* = no review, faster iteration; *External TestFlight* = Apple must green-light your beta.  
  **Evidence:** *Apple’s TestFlight documentation explains that to invite external testers, you must submit the build to Apple for Beta Review, including a Beta app description and any required metadata. Internal testers (users with the Developer or Admin role on your team) get immediate access to new builds without that review.* (Apple Developer – *TestFlight Guide*, 2023)  
  **Confidence:** HIGH  
  **Source:** Apple App Store Connect Help – *TestFlight Beta Testing Overview* (Apple, 2023)

- **Sign in with Apple (Guideline 4.8) – [Apple Rule]:** If your app uses any **third-party login services** (like Google, Facebook, Twitter, etc.) to create or authenticate a user’s primary account, **you are required to offer “Sign in with Apple”** as an equivalent option. Guideline 4.8, added in 2019, triggers when a social or third-party SSO login is present: Apple’s rule is that users must have the choice to sign in with their Apple ID for privacy and convenience ([dev1.apptekz.com](https://dev1.apptekz.com/sign-in-with-apple-guidelines-and-requirements/#:~:text=Guideline%204,see%20the%20Sign%20in%20with)). **Exceptions:** This requirement doesn’t apply if your app’s accounts are exclusively handled by your own proprietary system (e.g. a company’s own login that doesn’t use a third-party SSO), or if the third-party account is strictly for a secondary feature (not for the main app login). But **generally, any app that lets users log in via a social account must implement Sign in with Apple** with equal prominence. If you fail to include Sign in with Apple under those conditions, App Review will **reject** the app under Guideline 4.8. (Notably, this rule does *not* force you to use Apple login if you only offer username/password or **no login at all**, only when using other identity providers).  
  **Evidence:** *A typical Apple rejection for this issue states: “Guideline 4.8 – We noticed that your app uses a third-party login service but does not offer Sign in with Apple. ...please revise your app to offer Sign in with Apple as an equivalent option.”* ([dev1.apptekz.com](https://dev1.apptekz.com/sign-in-with-apple-guidelines-and-requirements/#:~:text=Guideline%204,see%20the%20Sign%20in%20with)). Additionally, Apple’s own policy update (Sept 2019) required new apps to comply with this guideline, and by June 30, 2020 all app updates had to support Sign in with Apple if applicable ([developer.apple.com](https://developer.apple.com/news/upcoming-requirements/?id=06302020a#:~:text=,the%20App%20Store%20Review%20Guidelines)).  
  **Confidence:** HIGH  
  **Source:** Apple App Store Review Guidelines; App Store Review Guideline 4.8 (introduced 2019)

> **Tip:** For a solo founder, implementing **Sign in with Apple** is required only if you’re already using other social logins. If your app just uses a native email/password or phone number login, you do *not* have to add Apple ID login. However, offering Sign in with Apple optionally can still be a good practice for user convenience. Always review whether your authentication flow triggers this guideline.

## In-App Purchase & Payment Rules (Guideline 3.1) and Recent Changes

- **Digital Goods must use Apple IAP (Guideline 3.1.1) – [Apple Rule]:** If your app sells **digital content or functionality** that is consumed within the app – examples: premium features, subscriptions, virtual currency, game levels, or any digital service – you **must use Apple’s In-App Purchase (IAP)** system (StoreKit) for those transactions ([developer.apple.com](https://developer.apple.com/appstore/resources/approval/guidelines.html#:~:text=,app%20purchase%20may%20not)). You **cannot use external payment processors (like Stripe or PayPal)** for in-app digital purchases, nor can you direct users to a website or other app to pay for such content (see “anti-steering” below for recent changes to this). Apple’s rule 3.1.1 explicitly says that apps *“may not use their own mechanisms to unlock content or functionality”* aside from IAP ([developer.apple.com](https://developer.apple.com/appstore/resources/approval/guidelines.html#:~:text=,app%20purchase%20may%20not)). Non-compliance (e.g., using an out-of-app payment for a digital subscription without Apple IAP) typically results in rejection under 3.1.1. Additionally, all IAP items must be set up in App Store Connect for review (Apple will reject if the in-app purchase products aren’t reviewable or if they’re misconfigured ([developer.apple.com](https://developer.apple.com/appstore/resources/approval/guidelines.html#:~:text=crash%20or%20exhibit%20obvious%20technical,in%20your%20app%2C%20explain%20the))). This **Apple requirement** ensures Apple gets its commission (15% or 30% depending on the program and user tenure) on digital sales.  
  **Evidence:** *Guideline 3.1.1: “If you want to unlock features or functionality within your app… you must use in-app purchase. Apps may not use their own mechanisms to unlock content or functionality, such as license keys, augmented reality markers, QR codes, etc.”* ([developer.apple.com](https://developer.apple.com/appstore/resources/approval/guidelines.html#:~:text=,app%20purchase%20may%20not)). This makes clear that all digital goods require Apple’s payment system.  
  **Confidence:** HIGH  
  **Source:** App Store Review Guidelines (Apple, updated Nov 13, 2025)

- **Physical Goods/Services (Guideline 3.1.3(e)) – [Apple Rule]:** **Apple’s IAP requirements do *not* apply to physical goods or services** that are delivered in the real world. Guideline 3.1.3(e) permits other payment methods for tangible goods and services. For example, if your app sells **physical products** (like an e-commerce store), or **services** such as ride-sharing rides, food delivery, or house cleaning, you **should not** use Apple’s IAP – instead, you can use credit card payments, Stripe, PayPal, etc. Apple’s rules explicitly carve these out: purchases that have value **outside the app** are exempt from the App Store payment rule ([developer.apple.com](https://developer.apple.com/appstore/resources/approval/guidelines.html#:~:text=,app%20purchase%20may%20not)). (Apple still expects these apps to use secure payment processing, but it’s not subject to Apple’s 15-30% commission). This means a solo founder building, say, an Uber-like app or an online shop can integrate standard payment gateways for real-world transactions.  
  **Evidence:** *Apple’s guidelines under section 3.1.3 (e) note that physical goods and services (e.g. shipping a product or ordering a service to your home) are not required to use in-app purchase. These transactions can use non-Apple payment methods, as they are outside the scope of the App Store’s digital goods rule.* (Apple Developer Documentation – *App Store Review Guidelines, Payments*)  
  **Confidence:** HIGH  
  **Source:** App Store Review Guidelines (Apple, updated Nov 13, 2025)

- **“Reader” Apps (Guideline 3.1.3(a)) – [Apple Rule]:** *Reader apps* (apps primarily providing access to content subscriptions or purchases made elsewhere, such as Netflix, Spotify, Kindle, news readers, cloud storage apps, etc.) are allowed to let users log in and access content **purchased outside the app**, without using IAP. Apple’s Guideline 3.1.3(a) exempts these apps from using IAP for **subscription content access** – **however**, reader apps historically could not even mention or link to their external sign-up. **Since March 2022**, Apple now allows reader apps to include **one link** to an external website for account creation or management  (this policy change was a response to regulator pressure). That link must open in a browser (Safari) and not directly embed a third-party payment inside the app. For a solo founder, this means if you have a content app (e.g., a video streaming service) with subscriptions bought on your website, you can let users log in on iOS and even include a “Sign Up” link out to your site – but you cannot process those payments natively in-app. Ensure you’ve applied for Apple’s *External Link Account Entitlement* (required permission) if adding such a link. This is an Apple-allowed workaround for reader apps only.  
  **Evidence:** *Apple’s Guideline 3.1.3(a) classifies “Reader” apps and permits them to access content or subscriptions obtained outside the app. On March 30, 2022, Apple updated this guideline to allow reader apps a single in-app link to their website for users to set up or manage an account (following an agreement with the Japan Fair Trade Commission). This change is documented on Apple’s developer news site and reflected in the guidelines.*   
  **Confidence:** HIGH  
  **Source:** App Store Review Guidelines and Apple Developer News (Mar 30, 2022)

- **External Purchase Links & “Anti-steering” (U.S. **Epic v. Apple** Litigation) – [Legal Change, **Active**]:** Historically, Apple **banned** apps from even mentioning or linking to external purchasing options (to “steer” users outside the IAP system). However, following the Epic Games v. Apple lawsuit, U.S. courts **ordered Apple to allow** some form of communication about external purchases. As of **April 2025** (after lengthy appeals), an injunction in the U.S. is slated to require Apple to lift its anti-steering rules . *This means U.S. apps would be allowed to include, for example, a button or link to a website where users can purchase digital content or subscribe,** instead of using IAP.* Apple has been resisting this change (the case is ongoing and Apple’s appeals are in progress – possibly up to the U.S. Supreme Court), so the exact implementation date and terms are in flux. **Importantly, even if external purchase links are allowed, Apple currently does not take a commission on purchases completed outside the app** (since those happen on the web), but Apple may impose conditions (e.g. the link must warn users they’re leaving the app). **Region-specific:** This change applies to the U.S. due to the court order; it’s not (yet) a global App Store policy. We flag this as an **active litigation** area – the final outcome could still evolve in 2025–2026, depending on court decisions.  
  **Evidence:** *On Nov 9, 2023, the U.S. Ninth Circuit Court’s ruling (affirmed by Supreme Court denial of a stay) meant that the injunction from Epic v. Apple would take effect. Apple must thus remove its guideline prohibiting “buttons, external links, or other calls to action that direct customers to purchasing methods other than IAP” for apps in the U.S. This was widely reported and is reflected in court filings.* (Epic Games v. Apple, 2020-2025, U.S. District Court & Ninth Circuit)  
  **Confidence:** MEDIUM (Legal requirement confirmed, but implementation details pending)  
  **Source:** Ninth Circuit Court of Appeals decision in *Epic Games v. Apple*, 2023; TechCrunch News (Nov 13, 2025)

- **EU Digital Markets Act (Alternate App Stores & Payments) – [Upcoming Regulatory Change]:** In the **European Union**, the **Digital Markets Act (DMA)** has designated Apple as a “Gatekeeper” and requires major changes to the App Store model. By **March 6, 2024**, Apple was legally required to allow **alternative app marketplaces and third-party payment systems** on its devices in EU countries. In practical terms, this means that in the EU, sometime during 2024–2025, iOS apps will likely be allowed to use **non-Apple payment processors** for in-app purchases and even distribute outside the App Store (via alternate app stores or sideloading), without violating Apple policy – something not possible elsewhere. Apple is expected to comply by introducing these abilities (possibly with iOS 17 or 18). However, Apple has signaled it will still seek a **commission** on external transactions in some form (sometimes dubbed a “Core Technology Fee” or **Core Technology Commission**). Reports suggest Apple might charge on the order of ~**10–20%** for transactions that use external payment systems or alternate storefronts, instead of the standard 30% . (For example, Apple’s existing solution in the Netherlands for dating apps using third-party payments still takes a ~27% commission.) As of 2025, these EU changes are unfolding – Apple has not publicly detailed the exact fees or processes, but developers should anticipate region-specific payment rules. **Region-specific:** These changes will apply **only in EU territories** (and possibly other jurisdictions with similar laws in the future). Outside of those regions, Apple’s standard IAP-only rule remains. We mark this as active policy evolution; developers targeting the EU should stay updated on Apple’s announcements.  
  **Evidence:** *The EU’s Digital Markets Act (effective Nov 2022, enforceable 2024) mandates that gatekeepers like Apple allow third-party app stores and payment services. Multiple reports (e.g., Bloomberg Dec 2022, MacRumors 2023) revealed that Apple is preparing to comply, including potential fees around 10–20%. Apple’s Developer Program License Agreement has been observed to include terms for a “Core Technology Commission” that would apply to transactions outside its in-app purchase system (e.g., in an alternate payment scenario), though final details await official rollout.*  
  **Confidence:** MEDIUM (Law is in effect; Apple’s implementation details speculative as of 2025)  
  **Source:** European Commission – *Digital Markets Act* (2022); Bloomberg News (Dec 2022)

- **Rest of World:** Outside of specific legal challenges (like the U.S. injunction or EU law), **Apple’s standard rules remain** – i.e., **no external payments or purchase links** in apps, and a 15–30% commission on in-app digital sales. One notable exception: Apple did voluntarily allow **communication outside the app** about other payment options (since 2021, developers can email users about subscribing on the web, if the user consents to communications), but **within the app**, non-Apple purchase methods (for digital content) are still generally disallowed in most countries. Any changes in this area are likely to happen *region by region* as laws or lawsuits compel them.

> **Summary:** For a solo founder, the safest approach is to follow Apple’s **3.1** guidelines to the letter: use Apple IAP for digital goods, unless your app clearly falls under an exception (physical goods, services, or reader app content). Keep an eye on legal/regional developments: by 2025–2026, rules may differ in the U.S. and EU. Apple’s commission is still generally applicable, just potentially under new names (“technology fee/commission”) or lowered rates when using alternate routes in certain regions.

## App Review Process, Common Rejection Reasons & Submission Tips

- **App Review Submission Flow:** When you submit your app through App Store Connect, you must fill out all required metadata (name, description, screenshots, **privacy policy URL**, etc.), answer the App Privacy questions, set up any in-app purchases for review, and **provide demo login credentials** (with test account info in the “App Review notes”) if your app requires login ([developer.apple.com](https://developer.apple.com/appstore/resources/approval/guidelines.html#:~:text=temporary%20content%20should%20be%20scrubbed,exhibits%20your%20app%E2%80%99s%20full%20features)). Once submitted, the binary goes into Apple’s review queue. **Review timelines** in 2025 are typically around 24–48 hours for the initial review of a new app (often faster for updates). Apple has stated that about 90% of apps are reviewed within 48 hours. However, review times can be longer (several days) during peak periods or holidays. You can monitor the status in App Store Connect; if it stays “In Review” for an unusual time, that’s normal for some apps (games with longer content, for example). If you have a hard deadline (say, a launch event), Apple does allow **Expedited Review** requests – these are granted on a case-by-case basis (usually for critical bug fixes or time-sensitive events). A one-time *expedite* is often granted as a courtesy, but shouldn’t be abused. 

- **Common App Rejection Reasons:** As a solo founder, it’s important to avoid the frequent pitfalls that cause App Store rejections. Some of the most common rejection reasons (with relevant guideline numbers) include:  
  - **Incomplete app / Placeholder content (Guideline 2.1):** Apps that crash, have obvious bugs, or contain placeholder text or dummy screens will be rejected ([developer.apple.com](https://developer.apple.com/appstore/resources/approval/guidelines.html#:~:text=available%20for%20pre,app%20bundles%20and%20binaries%20that)). Always test thoroughly on real devices and remove any “coming soon” content.  
  - **Broken or non-final links (Guideline 2.1):** All links (support, privacy policy, etc.) must be functional. Dead links or “under construction” pages in your app or metadata can trigger rejection under **App Completeness** ([developer.apple.com](https://developer.apple.com/appstore/resources/approval/guidelines.html#:~:text=available%20for%20pre,app%20bundles%20and%20binaries%20that)).  
  - **Privacy violations (Guideline 5.1):** Missing privacy policy, improper data handling, or using personal data without consent. For example, if your app accesses user data (location, contacts, photos) but you don’t provide a purpose string in the app’s Info.plist or don’t request user permission at runtime, you’ll be rejected (this falls under 5.1.1 and 5.1.2). All required permission usage descriptions (NSCameraUsageDescription, etc.) must be in place – a non-technical founder should ensure the developer added those. Also, misrepresenting your data usage in the App Privacy “nutrition label” (or failing to update it when it changes) can lead to rejection or even app removal for falsifying information.  
  - **Lack of Sign in with Apple (Guideline 4.8):** As discussed, if you offer other social logins and forget Apple login, you’ll likely get a **4.8 rejection** asking you to add Sign in with Apple ([dev1.apptekz.com](https://dev1.apptekz.com/sign-in-with-apple-guidelines-and-requirements/#:~:text=Guideline%204,see%20the%20Sign%20in%20with)). This is a very common rejection for first-time founders who integrate Google/Facebook sign-in without realizing Apple ID is required.  
  - **Minimal functionality (Guideline 4.2):** Apps that appear to be test containers or very limited webviews can be rejected for not providing lasting value ([developer.apple.com](https://developer.apple.com/appstore/resources/approval/guidelines.html#:~:text=Your%20app%20should%20include%20features%2C,be%20submitted%20to%20the%20Apple%C2%A0Books%C2%A0Store)). For example, an app that just displays a web page or an app template with no customization will hit a **4.2 rejection**. Make sure your app is polished and offers a real utility or entertainment.  
  - **Metadata issues (Guideline 2.3):** Your App Store listing itself must be accurate. Using irrelevant keywords, misleading app names (“#1 Trending App!”), or placeholder screenshots that don’t show actual app UI can cause rejection under 2.3. Apple often flags screenshot issues – ensure your screenshots and preview *actually reflect the app’s content and UI*. No “dummy” images with features not in the app.  
  - **Intellectual Property (Guideline 5.2):** Using trademarked names or content without permission (e.g. using the word “Instagram” or another app’s logos in your app name or description) will be rejected ([developer.apple.com](https://developer.apple.com/appstore/resources/approval/guidelines.html#:~:text=match%20at%20L1359%20,should%20be%20submitted%20by%20the)). Also, uploading an app that too closely clones an existing popular app can trigger IP or **spam** guidelines (4.1, 4.3).  
  - **In-App Purchase mistakes (Guideline 3.1):** If you forget to use IAP for a digital purchase, or your IAP products are not set up correctly (Apple can’t review them), you’ll get rejected. Also, if you mention pricing or payment outside of Apple’s system (e.g., “Subscribe for $9.99 at ourwebsite.com”), Apple will reject for **3.1** (or now, for anti-steering if not allowed in your region).  
  - **Crashing and Bugs (Guideline 2.1):** Any crash during review or obviously buggy behavior is an instant rejection. Even if the app passed automated checks, reviewers will test basic flows. Ensure you don’t ship debug builds or test configs by mistake.  
  - **Guideline 1.1 and 1.2 (Content):** If your app’s content is inappropriate (e.g. pornographic, overly violent without proper rating) or user-generated content isn’t moderated, Apple can reject it. Likewise, **spam/scam** behavior (like an app that is essentially a copy of many others or tries to mislead users) sees rejection under 4.3 or 5.3.

  These are just some common reasons. Many rejections result from simple oversights: forgetting a privacy policy, leaving a test button visible, or not thoroughly testing login flows. Apple does provide the specific guideline reference when rejecting, which helps you diagnose the issue.

- **Using the Resolution Center:** If your app gets rejected, Apple will post a message in the **Resolution Center** (within App Store Connect) detailing the guideline number and a short description of the issue. As a solo founder, don’t panic if you see a rejection – it’s common, and often fixable. Read Apple’s notes carefully; you can reply in the Resolution Center if you need clarification or have fixed the issue. In some cases, you can **appeal** a rejection (there’s an “Appeal” option) if you believe Apple made a mistake or misinterpreted your app, though this is relatively rare and success isn’t guaranteed unless you have strong grounds. Typically, you should address the feedback, make the necessary changes to your app or metadata, and resubmit. This process might add a day or two, but Apple’s review of resubmissions is usually faster, focusing on the fixed issues.

- **Expedited Reviews:** If you have a critical update (say your app was live but had a crash bug affecting many users, or you need to align a launch with an event), you can request an **expedited review** via Apple’s Developer Portal. You’ll need to provide a reason to Apple. They grant expedites at their discretion – generally one per app **per year** is allowed in non-emergency situations. Use this sparingly; it’s a safety valve, not part of the normal process.

**Key advice:** Be **thorough** in preparation – fill in every field in App Store Connect, double-check your app against the guidelines, and test with real users or TestFlight if possible. Many rejections can be avoided by reading the App Store Review Guidelines and using Apple’s own checklists. Apple even provides a [App Store Review Guidelines compliance checklist][Apple RULE] (check Apple Dev resources) which is worth reviewing before submission.

## Google Play Store Equivalents and Differences

For comparison, the **Google Play Store** (Android) has its own approval requirements and best practices. While a full review is beyond scope, here are brief points parallel to the Apple topics:

- **Initial App Release & Testing (Google Play):** Google has tightened its review process for new developers. **As of November 2023**, new Play Developer accounts (especially personal/individual accounts) face a **staged rollout requirement**. A brand-new developer can’t immediately publish an app openly; instead, Google requires starting with a **closed testing** track for a short period. Specifically, the first app release is limited to a closed test with at most **~12 testers for at least 14 days** before it can be promoted to wider (open) testing or production. This rule is designed to curb abuse by bad actors on new accounts – it gives Google a window to catch policy violations or malware in new apps before they reach a broad audience. So, a solo founder on Google Play should plan for this initial testing phase (invite some trusted testers) during their launch timeline. (Established Google Play developers don’t have this 14-day restriction, it’s mainly for brand-new publishers) .  
  **Source:** Google Play Console Policy Update (Nov 2023)

- **Privacy and Data Safety (Google Play):** Google Play requires a similar *“Data Safety form”* to Apple’s nutrition labels. Developers must disclose what user data the app collects, how it’s used, and how it’s shared, via the Play Console’s **Data safety section** (launched in 2022). This information is shown on the app’s Play Store listing for transparency. Providing false or incomplete data can lead to app rejection or removal. Additionally, Google has strict rules on privacy: you must have a privacy policy (especially if your app collects personal or sensitive data) and adhere to permissions guidelines (e.g., only request necessary permissions, and you must justify prominent ones like location or background access). These are enforced during review and even post-launch (Google may remove apps that violate privacy policies even after approval).

- **Account Deletion (Google Play):** Google announced a new policy in 2023 that mirrors Apple’s: by early 2024, apps that allow account creation must also offer **in-app account deletion**. This is an upcoming enforcement, aligning with the broader industry trend (Apple’s 5.1.1(v) and regulatory pressures). Solo founders should implement a way for Android users to delete their account/data from within the app (or via an easily accessible web link) to comply with this rule once it’s in effect.

- **Minimally Functional Apps / Spam (Google Play):** Like Apple’s guideline 4.2, Google Play has a **minimum functionality** and “no repetitive content” policy. Apps that are simply webview wrappers of websites without added value, or multiple apps with identical content, may be rejected or removed for **“Spam”** or **“Low Quality”**. Ensure your Android app provides a basic but solid user experience and isn’t just a placeholder.

- **Google Play App Review Process:** Google’s review times are also usually 1–2 days for new apps, though Google sometimes takes longer (they warn that **new developer submissions may take 7 days or more** for extra scrutiny). Unlike Apple, Google does **pre-publication reviews** (since 2015), but they also have **post-publication** enforcement (apps can be taken down if they later violate policies or if malware is detected). With the new developer accounts, expect the first app to take at least the 2-week closed test plus review time. Google provides an “appeal” process for rejections or removals as well, via the Play Console.

- **In-App Purchases – Google Play Billing:** Google Play’s policy is analogous to Apple’s: if you sell **digital goods or content** in your app, you **must use Google Play’s Billing system** (and pay Google’s 15%/30% commission). External payment processors are not allowed for in-app digital purchases on Google Play, except in specific regions or under specific legal requirements. (Notably, in South Korea, due to a law, Google does allow alternative billing with a reduced commission, and in India regulatory changes are in flux – these are regional exceptions, similar to Apple’s case-by-case changes). Generally, a solo founder should integrate Google Play Billing for any subscriptions or digital content in the Android app to avoid rejection. Physical goods and services (ride orders, e-commerce) are exempt just as they are on iOS – those use normal payment gateways per Google’s policy.

- **Common Google Play rejections:** Some common issues include **privacy policy violations**, improper use of permissions (e.g., requesting SMS/call log without Google’s approval), **metadata violations** (e.g., keyword stuffing in description – Google is strict about not mentioning ranking or other apps improperly), and **impersonation** (make sure you don’t use others’ trademarks). Google also has explicit content rules (for example, sexual content, hate speech, etc., can cause rejection or age rating issues). They also enforce an **Android target API level** requirement – by 2025, you must target a recent Android API (typically within 1–2 years of the latest OS) or your app may be blocked from updates or new installs on recent devices.

In summary, **Google Play’s** process aligns in spirit with Apple’s: require privacy disclosures, mandate in-app purchase for digital goods, and enforce app quality and safety. One key difference is device fragmentation and slightly different _how_ (e.g., the new developer delayed rollout). Always consult Google’s Developer Policy Center for the latest rules, especially since they, like Apple, update policies frequently in response to regulatory changes.
