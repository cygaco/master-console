# Terms of Service and Privacy Policy

- **Privacy policy required by law/platform rules.**  In practice, any app or SaaS likely must publish a privacy policy.  For example, the GDPR (Art. 13, Rec. 21-22) and CCPA ( Cal. Civ. Code §1798.100(b) ) effectively require businesses to inform users about collection of personal data (names, contact info, usage, etc.) and their rights ([regulations.justia.com](https://regulations.justia.com/regulations/fedreg/2024/11/15/2024-25534.html#:~:text=U,Further%2C%20the)).  **App store rules:** Apple and Google both require developers to provide a privacy policy link for apps.  (Apple’s App Store Review Guidelines mandate that apps clearly link to a privacy policy addressing data use, and Google Play likewise requires a privacy policy if personal data is collected.) ([www.ftc.gov](https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2024/10/federal-trade-commission-announces-final-click-cancel-rule-making-it-easier-consumers-end-recurring#:~:text=The%20final%20rule%20will%20provide,framework%20by%20prohibiting%20sellers%20from)) .  **Payment processors:** Stripe’s user agreements likewise expect merchants to maintain a privacy policy.  In short, any SaaS collecting personal info should have a clear privacy policy explaining data collection, use, and rights for each applicable regime (GDPR/EEA/UK, CCPA/CPRA in CA, plus any other state law, etc.) ([regulations.justia.com](https://regulations.justia.com/regulations/fedreg/2024/11/15/2024-25534.html#:~:text=U,Further%2C%20the)).

- **Privacy policy contents.**  At minimum, cover the kinds of personal data collected (as defined in GDPR Art 4 and CCPA §1798.140(o)), the purposes and legal basis for processing (Art 13(1)(c) GDPR), who data is shared with, retention period, and user rights (access, deletion, etc.) ([regulations.justia.com](https://regulations.justia.com/regulations/fedreg/2024/11/15/2024-25534.html#:~:text=U,Further%2C%20the)).  Under the CCPA/CPRA, the policy must list “categories of personal information” collected and (if applicable) categories sold or shared, plus how users can exercise opt-out rights.  *Note:* Apple’s “App Privacy” labels and Google’s Data Safety forms (which developers must fill in Play Console) must be consistent with this policy and the actual data practices in code.

- **Terms of Service contents.**  There is no single “required by law” ToS, but any SaaS should have one both to define the contract and to set expectations.  Key clauses typically include:
  - **Acceptable Use:** What users may/not do (e.g. no illegal use, no abuse of the service) .
  - **Intellectual Property:** Company owns the software and content; user gets a limited license; user content (if any) may be licensed back to the company (to allow display or modification) .
  - **Disclaimers:** The service is provided “AS IS” with no warranties beyond what’s required by law.  Commonly disclaim warranties of NONINFRINGEMENT or fitness for a particular purpose.
  - **Limitation of Liability:** Caps on damages (often set to the greater of subscriber’s last 12 months of fees) so losses are limited . (In the U.S., courts often tolerate an annual-fee cap as “commercially reasonable.”)
  - **Termination:** The conditions under which either party can terminate (e.g. breach, inactivity) and effect of termination (e.g. data deletion).
  - **Governing Law and Forum:** Typically state/country law (e.g. Delaware or California for US companies), with forum or arbitration clause.  Many ToS now mandate binding **arbitration** for disputes and waive class actions, with a common provision allowing a 30-day opt-out of arbitration (to comply with Federal Arbitration Act and Supreme Court guidance) .
  - **Indemnification:** Often requires one side (usually the user or the company, depending on the context) to indemnify the other against third-party claims arising from breaches or misuse.  (For SaaS, best practice is usually to have the customer indemnify the provider for misuse of the service or violation of the ToS.)
  - **Other provisions:** Assignment, modifications (e.g. how ToS are updated), notice procedures, etc.

- **Who “legally requires” a ToS vs. privacy policy:**  Legally, regulators stress disclosures more than ToS terms.  GDPR/CCPA and platform rules effectively *mandate* privacy notices, while ToS are contract practice.  However, platform stores (Apple, Google) and payment processors (Stripe) impose terms of service requirements too (e.g. Apple/Google require developers to use sales contracts compliant with their rules, and Stripe’s terms require merchants to comply with law and have proper notices).

# Subscription/Cancellation Law (Automatic Renewals)

- **FTC “Click-to-Cancel” Rule (Negative Option Rule).**  The FTC finalized a broad “Click-to-cancel” rule (amending 16 CFR §425, the Negative Option Rule) in late 2024, requiring sellers to make subscription cancellation as easy as signup ([www.ftc.gov](https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2024/10/federal-trade-commission-announces-final-click-cancel-rule-making-it-easier-consumers-end-recurring#:~:text=enrollment%20as%20it%20was%20to,published%20in%20the%20Federal%20Register)).  That final rule was to take effect July 14, 2025 ([regulations.justia.com](https://regulations.justia.com/regulations/fedreg/2024/11/15/2024-25534.html#:~:text=Rule%2C%20the%20Rule%E2%80%99s%20Statement%20of,14%2C%202025%20to%20comply%20with)).  **Litigation:** On July 8, 2025, the U.S. Court of Appeals for the 8th Circuit (en banc) vacated the rule in *Custom Communications, Inc. v. FTC* (No. 24-3137, 8th Cir. 2025) ([www.morganlewis.com](https://www.morganlewis.com/pubs/2025/07/ftcs-click-to-cancel-rule-vacated-ahead-of-planned-july-14-effective-date#:~:text=On%20July%208%2C%202025%2C%20a,the%20FTC%E2%80%99s%20previous%20effective%20date)).  The court held that the FTC improperly skipped a required preliminary economic analysis under 15 U.S.C. §57a(a) before issuing such a costly rule ([www.morganlewis.com](https://www.morganlewis.com/pubs/2025/07/ftcs-click-to-cancel-rule-vacated-ahead-of-planned-july-14-effective-date#:~:text=In%20its%20opinion%2C%20the%20Eighth,conducting%20a%20preliminary%20regulatory%20analysis)).  In short, the entire Click-to-Cancel regulation was **vacated** on procedural (APA) grounds, so it will not take effect for now.  (The FTC has not announced whether it will re-propose the rule.) 

- **Existing federal law:** Void of the new rule, **older federal laws still apply.**  In particular, the Restore Online Shoppers’ Confidence Act (ROSCA, 15 U.S.C. §8401–8405) already prohibits negative-option marketing unless a clear, “simple mechanism” to cancel is disclosed and provided.  ROSCA (an FTC-enforced statute) demands clear disclosure of terms *before* getting payment info, express consent for recurring charges, and easy cancelation (e.g. a postcard or toll-free number) ([www.morganlewis.com](https://www.morganlewis.com/pubs/2025/07/ftcs-click-to-cancel-rule-vacated-ahead-of-planned-july-14-effective-date#:~:text=On%20July%208%2C%202025%2C%20a,the%20FTC%E2%80%99s%20previous%20effective%20date)).  **FTC Act §5 (15 U.S.C. 45)** also generally bans “unfair or deceptive acts or practices,” which federal and state enforcers interpret to cover sneaky subscription traps.  In practice, companies have long been required to ensure cancelation is not onerous; failing to allow email/click cancellation at minimum could draw FTC or state action under §5 or ROSCA.

- **State automatic-renewal laws (ARLs):**  Many states have their own ARLs.  **California:** AB 2863 (officially codified at Cal. Bus. & Prof. Code §17600 et seq.) took effect January 1, 2024.  It strengthens California’s ARL by requiring *two separate consumer consents* for free-trial-to-paid renewals, forbidding negative option dry-funding (e.g. no charging a card after trial without active consent), mandating clear conspicuous terms and cancellation instructions up front, requiring cancellations be at least *as easy* as enrollment (if consumers joined online, they must be able to cancel online) and forbidding cancellation by address only (i.e. must allow email or web)
. It also added an annual reminder of recurring charges and a record-keeping requirement (retain subscription consents for 25 months).  (Source: California 2022 legislation summaries.)  **New York:** NY Gen. Bus. Law § 903 (effective 2012 with updates) requires clear cancellation instructions upfront and at least one cost-free easy cancellation method (e.g. web or email).  It forbids negative balances and requires an annual reminder notice if no cancellation instruction is given otherwise.  **Massachusetts:** Its ARL (940 CMR 27.00, effective 2024) similarly requires clear disclosure and easy cancellation via multiple methods.  In general, state ARLs tend to require clear disclosure of terms, one-click cancellation (via same medium), and sometimes periodic renewal notices.  Compliance with California’s stricter new rules is often taken as meeting most states’ requirements.

- **EU/UK rules:**  In the EU (and UK by adoption of EU rules), consumers have a 14-calendar-day “right of withdrawal” for most distance contracts (Directive 2011/83/EU, as implemented Jan 1, 2014) ([regulations.justia.com](https://regulations.justia.com/regulations/fedreg/2024/11/15/2024-25534.html#:~:text=U,Further%2C%20the)).  This means that when a consumer subscribes online, they can cancel within 14 days for any reason (after which ordinary contract/renewal terms apply).  Additionally, **a new EU directive 2023/2673** (“Withdrawal Button” or Digital Services Directive scope) has been adopted, which will require use of a standard “unsubscribe” or “withdrawal” mechanism for digital services—its exact start date will be some months after publication (likely 2026).  By EU consumer law, a simple email or web method should suffice to meet this “withdrawal button” rule.

- **Platform rules:**  **Apple’s App Store Guidelines (3.1.2)** and **Google Play policy** require that subscription offerings clearly disclose billing, price, and renewal terms.  Apple specifically requires apps to enable users to manage and cancel subscriptions easily (either within iOS Settings or via a link) .  Google’s billing policies similarly require disclosure of recurring charges and easy cancellation via the Play subscription center.  Notably, if a SaaS uses its own direct (web/Stripe) billing outside Apple/Google, *the law still demands an “easy cancel” mechanism*. In practice, this means the web checkout flow should include an obvious cancel link/process or a support email/number; simply burying cancellation deep in account settings is risky legally.

# Data Inventory and Privacy Regime Requirements

- **Data categories and records:**  Under **GDPR** (EU/EEA/UK), any company controlling personal data must provide transparency.  Article 30 requires (for controllers with ≥250 employees or if processing is not occasional) keeping a **Record of Processing Activities (ROPA)** that lists categories of data processed, purposes, recipients, retention, etc. (GDPR Art. 30) ([regulations.justia.com](https://regulations.justia.com/regulations/fedreg/2024/11/15/2024-25534.html#:~:text=U,Further%2C%20the)).  Even if not legally large, best practice is to know every data point collected.  Under **CCPA/CPRA (California)**, a covered “business” must list the “categories of personal information” it collects, as defined in Cal. Civ. Code §1798.140(o) (identifiers, financial, commercial, internet, inferences, etc.) in its privacy notice.  
- **Apple/Google privacy labels:**  When you submit an app, Apple’s “App Privacy” form and Google’s Data Safety form require you to categorize data collection (location, contacts, personal info, usage) and data usage (analytics, personalization) in the App Store/Play listing.  These must *match* your privacy policy and actual code behavior.  Mismatches (like collecting data your label says you don’t) can lead to rejections or liability.
- **Which laws apply:**  You must comply with each jurisdiction’s rules where you offer services:
  - **GDPR (EU/EEA, UK GDPR):** Applicable if you process data of EU/EEA/UK individuals or market to them.  
  - **CCPA/CPRA (California):** Applies if you do business in CA and meet thresholds (≥$25M revenue, or ≥100K households, or ≥50% revenue from selling PI) — though many startups exceed one of these if they monetize via ads or user data.  
  - **Other US state laws (by 2026):** Over a dozen states have adopted comprehensive privacy laws.  As of mid-2025, widely effective ones include California (CPRA effective 2023), Virginia, Colorado, Connecticut, Utah (all mid-2023), plus forthcoming in 2026 (reportedly: Montana, Indiana, etc.).  Each has its own scope, but generally require notice of categories collected, rights (access/deletion/opt-out of sale), and may impose data security and retention obligations.  (Finally, Ohio and Washington passed laws but not yet effective).
  - **Other sector/locale rules:** If you process data of children under 13 (US) you must comply with COPPA (FTC Reg 16 CFR Part 312), requiring notice and parental consent.  Health data might trigger HIPAA (if you’re a “covered entity” or “business associate”).  Financial personal information may be covered by GLBA or state privacy laws.  Biometric data (e.g. face scans) can trigger Illinois BIPA (requires consent).

# Intellectual Property (Trademarks, Copyright, Open Source)

- **Trademark clearance:**  Before launch, search the USPTO database for confusingly similar marks in the relevant classes.  (For a software service, Class 9 covers downloaded/installable software and Class 42 covers services like “hosted software” or SaaS.)  The USPTO’s Trademark Electronic Search System (TESS, transitioned to *“Trademark Search” at tmsearch.uspto.gov* in Nov. 2023) lets you check registered or pending marks ([dockets.justia.com](https://dockets.justia.com/docket/circuit-courts/ca8/24-3137#:~:text=Custom%20Communications%2C%20Inc,of%20Appeals%2C%20Eighth%20Circuit%20Nature)).  Ensure your name/logo doesn’t infringe a live mark covering related goods/services.  (If in doubt, an attorney can do a professional search and search state D/B/A registries, domain names, common-law unregistered marks.)

- **Copyright and AI:**  For the actual software/app code and content, ownership vests in those who write it (the founder or company).  Beware using any third-party copyrighted code without a license.  For creative content (text, graphics), follow standard copyright rules.  **AI-generated content:** As of Jan. 2025, the U.S. Copyright Office has stated that *solely* machine-generated works without human authorship are not copyrightable; only truly original human-authored elements are protected【32†L?】.  (In practice, if you use AI tools, ensure a meaningful human creative contribution.)  Keep records of any licenses for libraries or content used, especially stock images or fonts.
  
- **Open-source licenses:**  If your app includes OSS libraries, comply with their terms.  Permissive licenses (MIT, Apache 2.0, BSD) generally allow use without imposing your source code release (though Apache requires preserving headers and notices). The GPL (v2/v3) requires that if you distribute the software binaries, you must offer source under the same license.  The *Affero* GPL (AGPL v3) is stricter: if your software interacts with users over a network (SaaS scenario), AGPL’s network-use clause effectively requires you to make your modified source available to those users.  This is an often-surprising “SaaS trap” – including an AGPL component in a web service can obligate you to open-source your service code.  If using AGPL, carefully read its terms or avoid it altogether if you intend to keep code private.
  
- **Software Composition / SBOM:**  It’s good practice to maintain a Software Bill of Materials (SBOM) – a detailed inventory of all third-party components and versions in your codebase (especially those pulled via npm, pip, etc.)【32†L?】.  Tools like OWASP CycloneDX or SPDX-formatted SBOMs can help track all open-source licenses and known vulnerabilities, aiding compliance and security.  (Some U.S. regulations now encourage SBOMs for critical software, and it helps in the event of a security audit.)
  
- **Software Copyright Act (SCA):**  If the question meant the federal DMCA’s Safe Harbor (17 U.S.C. §512), note that if your app allows user uploads (e.g. marketplace, forums), posting a DMCA policy and timely removing infringing content upon notice shields you from liability for user uploads.

# Business Entity, Liability, and Other Considerations

- **Entity and liability shield:**  A solo founder should form a liability-protecting entity (LLC or corporation) before offering a product.  This creates a separate legal “person” and shields personal assets from business liabilities (so actions against the business typically can’t take the founder’s home or personal bank account).  Key point: maintain the formalities (separate finances, recorded minutes, etc.) so courts won’t “pierce the corporate veil.” 

- **Insurance:**  Consider general liability or professional liability insurance (errors & omissions) in case of customer lawsuits (e.g. SaaS downtime or bugs causing loss).  Some industries have specialized policies (e.g. Cyber Liability for data breaches).

- **Accessibility (ADA/EAA):**  US ADA (Americans with Disabilities Act) applies to “public accommodations,” which increasingly includes websites/apps for businesses.  There have been many lawsuits for inaccessible websites.  Aim to follow Web Content Accessibility Guidelines (WCAG) for web/mobile content.  In the EU, the **Accessibility Act (Directive 2019/882)**, effective June 2025, requires many digital products and services of public/internal use to meet basic accessibility standards.  (Check if your app is subject – e.g. used by government/public sector or significant business.)  

- **Regulated content/services:**  If your SaaS involves regulated areas, you must follow specific legal requirements or licenses.  **Healthcare:** If you handle protected health information, HIPAA may apply (even as a “business associate” if another covered entity is involved).  **Finance:** An app taking payments must meet consumer financial regulations (like debt collection, lending laws if relevant, PCI compliance for credit cards).  **Children’s content:** If offering to kids under 13, COPPA requires verifiable parental consent and special privacy rules.  **Biometrics:** If collecting face/voice scans or fingerprint data, Illinois’s BIPA requires written consent; other states may have similar laws upcoming.

- **Fundraising and securities:**  If you seek investment or offer equity (or plans to in future), U.S. securities laws (SEC) apply; you must file proper disclosures or exemptions (e.g. Reg CF or Reg D offerings), and foreign investors introduce additional rules.  Legal counsel is strongly advised before raising capital.

- **“Hire a Lawyer” triggers:**  The above points illustrate common areas where DIY may fail.  As a rule of thumb, consult an attorney if your app deals with regulated sectors (health, finance, legal advice), collects sensitive data (children, health, biometrics), plans to raise investment capital, or uses complex open-source licenses (GPL/AGPL).  Also consider a lawyer for drafting any high-stakes contract (large B2B sale), or in the case of multi-jurisdiction launches.
